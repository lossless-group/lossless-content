# Wyss Institute

- ![Wyss Institute researchers working with human tissue cultures](https://wyss-prod.s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/app/uploads/2025/09/09152354/cropped-Michelle-Katharina-and-Jack-in-Tissue-Culture-Candid-05652_FINAL-1-scaled-1.jpg)
- _A Harvard-affiliated nonprofit research and development institute that turns biologically inspired science into technologies for human and planetary health._
- The Wyss Institute at Harvard University is a **nonprofit research and development organization** founded in 2009.[6] It operates in the Boston–Cambridge research ecosystem through collaborations with Harvard University, Harvard Medical School, Boston Children’s Hospital, and external partners.[6][8] Innovation consultants track it for its translational pipeline, which moves discoveries from academic laboratories toward real-world products and therapies.[6]

## Mission and Identity

> “The Wyss Institute at Harvard University is a nonprofit research and development organization dedicated to translating groundbreaking discoveries from the lab into real-world solutions for human and planetary health.” [6]

The institute says it leverages “Nature’s genius” to address urgent global challenges, including disease, medical treatment, and sustainability.[6][8] Its model emphasizes multidisciplinary collaboration among biologists, engineers, clinicians, and external partners to translate research into usable technologies.[6][9]

- **Operating principle:** Use biologically inspired engineering and collaborative research to convert laboratory discoveries into real-world solutions.[6]

## What They Do

The Wyss conducts translational research spanning medicine, engineering, materials, sustainability, and diagnostics.[6][8] Its teams develop enabling technologies, publish research, secure external funding, and license or otherwise advance promising inventions toward commercialization.[8][10]

- **Organ-on-chip research:** Human [[Organ-on-a-Chip Technology]] systems for modeling disease and testing therapies, including Lyme disease research.[8]
- **[[3D Organs]] engineering:** Biological inks and multimaterial 3D printing for vascularized tissues and organoids.[6]
- **DNA nanotechnology:** DNA nanoswitch platforms for drug discovery and protein-interaction screening.[10]
- **[[Precision Diagnostics]]:** Extracellular-vesicle methods intended to reveal biomarkers from brain and nervous-system cells.[9]
- **Sustainable materials and [[concepts/Biomanufacturing|Biomanufacturing]]:** Microbial systems designed to convert plastic waste into bioplastics.[5]
- **Biomedical device and therapeutic development:** Research spanning cancer therapies, neurological disease, cardiovascular disease, and other health applications.[6][7]

## Leadership and People

- [Donald Ingber](https://wyss.harvard.edu/team/leadership/donald-ingber/) — founding director — also holds professorships at Harvard Medical School, Boston Children’s Hospital, and Harvard’s John A. Paulson School of Engineering and Applied Sciences.[9][10]
- [Jennifer Lewis](https://wyss.harvard.edu/team/core-faculty/jennifer-lewis/) — core faculty member and leader of the 3D Organ Engineering Initiative — recognized for work in 3D bioprinting, vascularized organoids, and human tissues.[6]
- [Ernest Aguinam](https://wyss.harvard.edu/) — researcher associated with immune-system research addressing radiation injury and chronic Lyme disease.[2]
- [Silvi Lybbert](https://wyss.harvard.edu/news/humans-of-the-wyss-silvi-lybbert-on-turning-plastic-trash-into-bioplastic-treasure/) — researcher working on the SPEEDR plastic-degradation and bioplastic project.[5]

## History and Origin Story

The Wyss Institute was established at Harvard University in 2009 as a nonprofit research and development organization intended to translate biologically inspired discoveries into practical technologies.[6] Its founding model combines Harvard-based research with clinical, philanthropic, governmental, and commercial partnerships.[8][9]

- **2009** — The Wyss Institute was founded at Harvard University.[6]
- **2025–2026** — Bay Area Lyme Foundation funding enabled a Wyss project using personalized Lymphoid Organ Chips to investigate Lyme disease therapeutics.[8]
- **2026** — Jennifer Lewis was elected to the National Academy of Medicine for contributions to 3D bioprinting and engineered human tissues.[6]
- **2026** — The institute’s FIND DNA-nanoswitch drug-discovery technology was licensed to a startup co-founded by members of the research team.[10]
- **2026** — Wyss researchers reported a framework for isolating neuron-specific extracellular vesicles to support neurological biomarkers and precision diagnostics.[9]

## Milestones and Signature Output

- **Organ Chips for disease modeling** — 2010s–2026 — human Organ Chip platforms model disease biology and therapeutic responses, including personalized immune responses in Lyme disease.[8]
- **3D bioprinting of living tissues** — 2010s–2026 — Jennifer Lewis’s work advanced biological inks, vascularized organoids, and multimaterial printing for drug discovery, disease modeling, and tissue repair.[6]
- **FIND DNA-nanoswitch platform** — 2026 — screens molecules according to their effects on protein–protein interactions and was licensed to a startup.[10]
- **Neuron-specific extracellular-vesicle isolation** — 2026 — enables researchers to examine biomarker-loaded vesicles originating from brain cells in blood and cerebrospinal fluid.[9]
- **SPEEDR bioplastic system** — 2025–2026 — engineers a microbe to break down plastic waste and use resulting byproducts to make bioplastics.[5]
- **Translational health and sustainability pipeline** — since 2009 — the institute reports a portfolio ranging from cancer therapies to sustainable materials.[6]

- **Impact on society**
  - The institute’s Organ Chip research is aimed at improving understanding and treatment of Lyme disease by comparing immune responses from different patient donors.[8]
  - Its neurological extracellular-vesicle work could support earlier disease detection and treatment monitoring by exposing molecular signals from brain cells through blood or cerebrospinal-fluid samples.[9]

- **Impact on innovation**
  - The Wyss helped establish a translational model that combines biologically inspired engineering, human-relevant disease models, and commercialization partnerships.[6][8]
  - Its research has produced platforms spanning organ chips, living-tissue bioprinting, DNA nanoswitch screening, and biomarker isolation.[6][8][9][10]

- **Impact on its industry or domain**
  - Organ Chips offer a human-cell-based approach to studying disease and therapeutic response, while the FIND platform shifts drug screening toward functional effects on protein interactions rather than simple target binding.[8][10]
  - The institute’s 3D bioprinting work has expanded the technical possibilities for vascularized organoids and engineered human tissues.[6]
