Most market maps are flat. A grid of logos, a quadrant, a list of categories. They show you who exists. They can't show you what it feels like to be in the market: why some companies seem to get pulled upward, why others stall at the same stage for years, and why a single event can move a company further than three years of work did.

So here's a different picture. It's a thought experiment borrowed from physics, not a measured law. I've found it useful anyway.

Imagine value as a space, and every market as its own universe within it. Enterprise software, biotech, Berlin, Bangalore: each has its own hot center and its own rules, and they overlap in places. That's the multiverse. Every universe seems to share the same basic physics.

The things moving through this space are what I call **capital flow entities**, or CFEs: startups, funds, angels, corporate venture arms, anything capital moves into, out of or through.

## The Standard Model

**1. Value space has a shape.** Picture a band-based, concentric doughnut. A market isn't a smooth slope from small to big. It has zones, like seed-stage regional startups in an outer band and category leaders with top-tier investors near the center.

**2. Value capture follows a multidimensional Pareto curve.** The centers of value capture show exponentially greater "heat." This is the part with the most evidence. Correlation Ventures looked at more than 21,000 US financings from 2004 to 2013 and found that 65% failed to return the capital invested, and only 4% returned 10x or more. [^lhkknq] See also [[concepts/Pareto Curves|Pareto Curves]].

**3. There are "gravitational" and "magnetic" forces.** CFEs move closer to and further from the hot center, and they run into push and pull, repulsion and propulsion, from the statistical bands of normal distribution in each doughnut-shaped zone. There's evidence for this pull too. High-reputation VCs' offers are three times more likely to be accepted, and those firms buy startup equity at a 10–14% discount. [^w9uu89] Founders are literally paying to get closer to the heat.

**4. Forces change as you approach an edge.** In any zone, forces get stronger near the edge of the next zone, in inverse proportion to the gravity of the band's center. Being in the comfortable middle of a band is stable. Getting near the boundary is where things start to move fast, in either direction.

**5. External forces are the main propulsion.** A CFE can use momentum within external forces as its primary way to travel. Nobody fights their way from the outer band to the center on effort alone. A platform shift, a regulatory change or a hot category does most of the moving, and the skill is in catching it. [[essays/The AI Model Wars|The AI Model Wars]] are propelling a lot of companies right now that were nowhere two years ago.

**6. Leaps exist.** Various strategies and tactics can catalyze a "leap," which works as a shortcut through space-time. It skips a band instead of crossing it. Getting into [[vertical-toolkits/Alternatives/Venture-Capital-Firms/Y Combinator|Y Combinator]] can work this way, and so can a marquee customer, a lead from a top firm or an acquisition by a company in a hotter universe.

**7. Every object exerts force on every other.** As CFEs get closer together, cluster heat creates rapid and chaotic movement. Talent hops between companies. Investors crowd into the same rounds. Competitors copy each other within a quarter. Anyone who has watched a hot category at close range has seen how quickly the positions shuffle.

## Why Bother With a Model Like This?

Because it changes the questions you ask.

**If you're a founder,** "how do we grow?" becomes "which band are we in, and how close are we to its edge?" Being a good company in the wrong universe is a real condition, and it won't be fixed by working harder. It may be worth asking which force you're riding, and whether you're close enough to a boundary for a leap to land.

**If you're an investor,** a firm is a CFE too, with its own position and its own pull. Your brand exerts force on founders, whether you earned it or inherited it. Part of what you're selling is proximity.

**If you're building an ecosystem,** this explains [[essays/The Geography of Innovation|The Geography of Innovation]]. A new hub is trying to light a new center in its own universe, then hold enough heat that its CFEs stop leaving for the bigger one. That's why ecosystems are so hard to build from a standing start, and why accelerators and regional funds matter more than they appear to.

## Where the Metaphor Breaks

Physics metaphors deserve suspicion, and this one has limits. The heat in point 2 is measured. Points 3 and 6 have some support. The rest is an observation I can't yet quantify, and the bands themselves are a drawing, not data.

But [[essays/Technology wants to be Emergent|innovation is emergent]], and emergent systems are often better understood through their dynamics than through a list of participants. A flat market map tells you who's on the field. I want a way to talk about the forces acting on them.

If you've got a better model, or data that breaks this one, I'd like to see it.
