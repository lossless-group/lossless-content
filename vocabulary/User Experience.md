# Defining and Describing User Experience

![Side‑by‑side comparison of two mobile app screens, one cluttered and confusing, one clean and intuitive, with annotations about emotions, effort, and outcomes during use](https://blog.rheinwerk-computing.com/hs-fs/hubfs/ux_design_infograhpic.png?width=1275&height=2475&name=ux_design_infograhpic.png)

_*User experience (UX) is the overall quality of a person’s interaction with a product or service — including emotions, expectations, and outcomes — as they try to accomplish something they care about in a specific context.*_ [^32i8ju] [^bh29gm] [^b08uul] [^5ev9cq]

For innovation work, **user experience** applies whenever a human is trying to achieve a goal through a designed system — a SaaS dashboard, a marketplace, a fintech onboarding flow, a hardware device, or a service touchpoint. [^32i8ju] [^bh29gm] [^b08uul] It explicitly includes what happens *before, during, and after use*: expectations from the brand, the feeling while using it, and the decision to stay, churn, or advocate. [^32i8ju] [^t3w3ca] [^to6i8h] [^b08uul] It does **not** apply to purely internal engineering elegance or business strategy in the abstract unless those choices show up in what the user perceives and experiences. [^32i8ju] [^bh29gm] An innovation consultant cares about UX because it is often where product‑market fit is won or lost: users interpret value, trust, and differentiation through their lived experience of the product, not the founder’s intended design. [^32i8ju] [^bh29gm] [^5ev9cq]  

# Disambiguation

## Primary sense — the innovation-consulting sense

**User experience (UX) as an innovation lever**:  
In innovation and startup contexts, *user experience is the total, subjective perception and response a user has before, during, and after interacting with a product, system, or service, including emotions, beliefs, usability, effectiveness, and the broader context in which it is used.* [^32i8ju] [^t3w3ca] [^b08uul] [^uxx87a]

- **Scope: beyond “UI” or “usability”**  
  The ISO 9241‑210 standard defines user experience as a *“user’s perceptions and responses that result from the use and/or anticipated use of a system, product or service,”* explicitly including *“all the users’ emotions, beliefs, preferences, perceptions, physical and psychological responses, behaviors, and accomplishments that occur before, during, and after use.”* [^32i8ju] [^t3w3ca] [^to6i8h] [^t1elul] [^da5hzl] [^b08uul] This means UX covers information architecture, interaction flows, performance, content, and emotional tone — not just the visible interface. [^32i8ju] [^x8qvx5] [^1wkxie] [^bh29gm] [^b08uul] [^5ev9cq]

- **What this sense is NOT**  
  UX is **not** just visual design or branding; Norman coined the term at Apple in the early 1990s precisely to go *beyond* “interface design” and “industrial design” and cover “all aspects of the experience of a person with a system.” [^x8qvx5] [^1o5ei7] [^g3122o] [^vfs7ou] [^5ev9cq] It is also not fully controllable by the company: ISO‑aligned definitions emphasize UX as a *resulting phenomenon* shaped by the user’s prior experiences, attitudes, abilities, and the context of use, which organizations can influence but not dictate. [^t3w3ca] [^32i8ju] [^to6i8h] [^b08uul] [^uxx87a]

- **Common usage in startups and innovation**  
  Contemporary product and UX literature frames UX as *“the quality of experience a person has when interacting with a specific design”* and as the *“overall impression a person forms while interacting with a product, service, or system.”* [^x8qvx5] [^1wkxie] [^bh29gm] Startup‑oriented glossaries describe UX as “the total quality of a user's interaction with a product across usability, accessibility, performance, content, design, and emotional response,” emphasizing that the product is effectively *the experience* the user has. [^5ev9cq] For founders and product teams, UX becomes the operational bridge between problem understanding, solution design, and measurable user outcomes.

- **Boundary cases / nearby but different concepts**  
  UX overlaps with but is distinct from **usability**, which focuses on effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction in achieving specific goals; the UX concept was promoted in the mid‑1990s in HCI precisely to broaden attention beyond purely instrumental use and usability metrics. [^32i8ju] [^g3122o] It is also broader than **customer experience (CX)**, which spans the entire relationship across channels and lifecycle; ISO 9241‑210 and UX scholarship focus specifically on experiences that result from use or anticipated use of systems, products, and services. [^t3w3ca] [^32i8ju] [^g3122o] [^b08uul] Finally, UX is not the same as **business performance**: while better UX is often correlated with adoption and retention, standards bodies present UX as a human‑centric construct, not a commercial KPI. [^t3w3ca] [^32i8ju] [^g3122o] [^b08uul]

## Other senses

### 1. UX as a formal discipline / profession

**Definition**: The professional discipline of *user experience* refers to the design, research, and evaluation practices focused on shaping and assessing the quality of users’ experiences with interactive systems.  

- UX as a field emerged in **human–computer interaction (HCI)** and ergonomics, with interest in “user experience” becoming apparent in the mid‑1990s as advocates argued that design and evaluation should extend beyond instrumental use to include a broader range of experiential qualities. [^g3122o] [^32i8ju]  
- Don Norman and colleagues at Apple helped institutionalize UX as an organizational function through structures like the “User Experience Architect’s Office” and the “User Experience Requirements Document,” signaling a practice that spans research and design divisions. [^g3122o] [^1o5ei7] [^vfs7ou]  
- Today, glossaries and education sites describe UX work as encompassing user research, interaction design, information architecture, prototyping, and evaluation methods aimed at influencing the perceptions and responses defined in ISO 9241‑210. [^32i8ju] [^x8qvx5] [^amn73o] [^bh29gm] [^to6i8h]

### 2. UX as a marketing or branding shorthand

**Definition**: In marketing and corporate communications, *user experience* is often used as a broad label for perceived product quality, delight, or “polish,” sometimes detached from rigorous UX practice.  

- Many vendor and software marketing glossaries define UX in simplified, promotional terms such as “the entire subjective perception of a person before, during and after using a product,” tying it directly to brand success and competitive advantage. [^b08uul] [^bh29gm]  
- This sense tends to conflate UX with **customer satisfaction**, loyalty, or NPS, framing UX primarily as a differentiator or selling point rather than as a discipline grounded in HCI standards and empirical research. [^32i8ju] [^g3122o] [^b08uul]

- **Other fields**  
  The phrase *user experience* also appears in general consumer‑electronics reviews, gaming reviews, or even physical‑space design (e.g., “museum user experience”), but these uses are typically informal extensions of the primary sense and add little new meaning for innovation contexts. [^32i8ju]

# Etymology and Origin

- **Coinage and early use**  
  Multiple historical overviews credit **Donald Norman**, a cognitive scientist and designer, with coining or at least deliberately *choosing* the term **“user experience”** while working at Apple in the early 1990s. [^x8qvx5] [^1o5ei7] [^g3122o] [^vfs7ou] [^5ev9cq] Norman has described how he “invented the title of User Experience Architect” at Apple because he wanted a term that covered “all aspects of the experience of a person with a system,” not just interface or industrial design. [^x8qvx5] [^1o5ei7] [^vfs7ou]  

- **Institutionalization at Apple (mid‑1990s)**  
  A history of UX psychology notes that in 1995 Norman, Jim Miller, and Austin Henderson presented an overview of their work at Apple, describing “user experience” as their preferred alternative to “human interface research and application.” [^g3122o] This terminology was reflected in Apple’s “User Experience Architect’s Office” and the “User Experience Requirements Document,” which helped propagate the phrase through industry and academia. [^g3122o]

- **Standardization and migration into broader business vocabulary**  
  The international ergonomics standard **ISO 9241‑210** (and its predecessor ISO 9241‑210:2010) formally defined user experience as “a user’s perceptions and responses that result from the use and/or anticipated use of a system, product or service,” explicitly listing emotions, beliefs, preferences, perceptions, physical and psychological responses, behaviors, and accomplishments. [^t3w3ca] [^32i8ju] [^to6i8h] [^t1elul] [^da5hzl] [^b08uul] As UX practice matured, this standard became a reference point for UX professionals, design education, and business literature, helping the term migrate from HCI into mainstream product management and innovation discourse. [^32i8ju] [^amn73o] [^1wkxie] [^bh29gm] [^to6i8h] [^b08uul] [^uxx87a]  

- **Recent reframings**  
  Contemporary UX writers continue to emphasize Norman’s contribution, framing him as the person “who gave the practice the title we use today” and highlighting his intent to capture the “whole encounter between a person and a thing.” [^vfs7ou] [^1o5ei7] [^g3122o] [^5ev9cq]

# Adjacent Vocabulary

- **Synonyms**
  - **User experience (UX)** vs. **user experience quality**: Some sources phrase UX as the **quality** of a person’s experience with a design, emphasizing that UX is not just any interaction but its *experienced quality* (e.g., pleasant, frustrating, effortless). [^x8qvx5] [^1wkxie] [^bh29gm]  
  - **User experience** vs. **user experience (UX) of a product/service/system**: ISO and derivative glossaries effectively use these interchangeably, defining UX as a person’s perceptions and responses resulting from use or anticipated use of a product, system, or service. [^t3w3ca] [^32i8ju] [^1wkxie] [^bh29gm] [^to6i8h] [^t1elul] [^da5hzl] [^b08uul]  
  - **User experience** vs. **user experience design**: Many industry sources colloquially conflate the experience itself with the practice of designing for it, but formally the experience is the user’s perception, while UX design is the set of activities aimed at influencing it. [^32i8ju] [^x8qvx5] [^amn73o] [^bh29gm] [^to6i8h] [^g3122o]

- **Antonyms**
  - **Poor user experience / bad UX**: While not formal antonyms, UX literature often contrasts good UX with experiences that are confusing, inefficient, or emotionally negative; in practice conversations, “bad UX” is used as the opposite of a high‑quality user experience. [^x8qvx5] [^amn73o] [^bh29gm]  
  - **Usability failure**: HCI texts discuss systems that are hard to use or ineffective in supporting user goals as exemplars of poor or failed user experience. [^32i8ju] [^g3122o]

- **Adjacent terms**
  - [[User interface]] — the visual and interactive layer through which the user interacts; UX encompasses but extends beyond UI. [^32i8ju] [^x8qvx5] [^bh29gm] [^5ev9cq]  
  - [[Usability]] — the effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction with which specified users achieve specified goals; a subset dimension within UX. [^32i8ju] [^t3w3ca] [^g3122o]  
  - [[Customer experience]] — the total experience across all interactions and touchpoints with a company, of which UX is one component. [^32i8ju] [^g3122o] [^b08uul]  
  - [[Human–computer interaction]] — the academic discipline from which UX as a concept and field emerged. [^32i8ju] [^g3122o] [^x8qvx5]  
  - [[Design thinking]] — a broader innovation process that often uses UX research and prototyping to explore desirability in human terms. [^32i8ju] [^amn73o] [^g3122o]  
  - [[Product management]] — the discipline that frequently integrates UX insights into decisions about features, prioritization, and strategy. [^32i8ju] [^bh29gm] [^5ev9cq]

# Usage in Practice

![Annotated screenshot of a founder presentation slide showing “UX” circled between “Customer Problem” and “Retention/Revenue” in a product funnel](https://blog.rheinwerk-computing.com/hs-fs/hubfs/image-png-Feb-05-2025-04-38-37-2530-PM.png?width=1185&height=712&name=image-png-Feb-05-2025-04-38-37-2530-PM.png)

- Phil Turner’s historical overview notes that advocates of user experience in the mid‑1990s proposed that “the design and evaluation of digital technology should be extended beyond the purely instrumental to include the broader range of experience,” capturing how UX reframed HCI focus from just task performance to holistic experience. [^g3122o]  
- A glossary of UX explains that “‘User Experience’, often abbreviated ‘UX’, is the quality of experience a person has when interacting with a specific design,” explicitly connecting the term to interaction quality rather than just feature presence. [^x8qvx5]  
- An ISO‑aligned explainer describes UX as “the overall impression a person forms while interacting with a product, service, or system,” emphasizing that UX is about the total impression, not isolated UI elements. [^1wkxie]  
- A business‑oriented UX glossary states that user experience “is the total perception a person forms while interacting with a product, encompassing usability, performance, emotional response, and the broader context of how the product fits into their workflow,” highlighting the role of context and workflow fit for work products. [^bh29gm]  
- A detailed standards‑based article notes that user experience “includes all emotions, beliefs, preferences, perceptions, physical and psychological reactions, behavior and achievement of the user arising before, during and after use of the system,” echoing the ISO 9241‑210 phrasing. [^to6i8h] [^t3w3ca] [^32i8ju] [^b08uul]  
- A product‑startup lexicon frames UX as “the total quality of a user's interaction with a product across usability, accessibility, performance, content, design, and emotional response,” clarifying that UX treats the product as what the user actually experiences, “not just the interface they see.” [^5ev9cq]  
- A Japanese‑language design glossary similarly defines UX as the “totality of subjective perceptions, emotions, meanings, and evaluations that arise through a user’s interaction with products, services, or organizations,” and notes that it is a *resulting phenomenon* that companies can influence but not directly control. [^uxx87a]

# Common Misuses

- **Equating UX with just “UI” (visual design)**  
  Misuse: Calling any work on colors, typography, or layout “UX,” as if UX were only about the visible interface.  
  Better term: **User interface (UI) design** — UX, per ISO and Norman’s intent, spans all perceptions and responses before, during, and after use, not just visual appearance. [^32i8ju] [^x8qvx5] [^to6i8h] [^b08uul] [^5ev9cq]

- **Reducing UX to “usability testing” or task efficiency**  
  Misuse: Treating UX as purely about making tasks efficient or running usability tests.  
  Better term: **Usability** — UX advocates in HCI explicitly broadened the scope beyond purely instrumental task success to encompass a wider experiential range. [^32i8ju] [^g3122o]

- **Using “UX” as a synonym for overall brand or customer relationship**  
  Misuse: Describing every interaction across marketing, sales, and support as “UX,” ignoring the more specific focus on experiences tied to system use or anticipated use.  
  Better term: **Customer experience (CX)** for the broader, multi‑channel relationship; UX is one part, focused on interactions with the product/service itself. [^32i8ju] [^g3122o] [^b08uul]

- **Treating UX as something fully controllable by the company**  
  Misuse: Marketing claims that companies “deliver” or “own” the user experience as if it were entirely under their control.  
  Better term: **User experience outcomes / perceptions** — ISO 9241‑210 and related interpretations describe UX as a *result* shaped by users’ internal states, prior experiences, and context; organizations can design for and influence UX but cannot guarantee a specific subjective experience. [^t3w3ca] [^32i8ju] [^to6i8h] [^b08uul] [^uxx87a]
