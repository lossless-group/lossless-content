# Defining and Describing Behavioral Sciences

- [IMAGE 1: Diagram linking psychology, sociology, anthropology, behavioral economics, biology, and organizational research to startup and innovation decisions]
- _In an innovation-consulting context, behavioral sciences are the evidence-based disciplines used to understand and influence how customers, employees, founders, and organizations actually behave._
- The term applies when a team investigates behavior through systematic observation, experimentation, and analysis rather than relying only on stated preferences or intuition.[^64j9h8] It is especially relevant to product adoption, customer decision-making, organizational change, incentives, leadership, and founder judgment.[^s52vys][^39vtxt] It does not mean simply collecting opinions, conducting a conventional market survey, or labeling a product “behavioral” without testing whether behavior changes.[^wzth9i]

# Disambiguation

## Primary sense — the innovation-consulting sense

Behavioral sciences are the cross-disciplinary application of psychology, sociology, economics, anthropology, and related empirical fields to explain, predict, and change behavior in innovation and organizational settings.[^64j9h8][^wzth9i]

- In product and market work, the focus is what people do under real conditions—such as whether they try, adopt, continue using, recommend, or abandon an offering—not merely what they say they intend to do.[^wzth9i]
- In technology adoption, behavioral analysis considers beliefs such as usefulness and ease of use alongside social influence, infrastructure, workflow, organizational structure, regulation, and incentives.[^s52vys]
- In organizational change, the practical question is often how to redesign the environment so desired actions become easier and more reliable, rather than simply telling people to change.[^39vtxt][^fuy2cw]
- Behavioral sciences are broader than behavioral economics: behavioral economics concentrates on economic decisions, while behavioral science also draws on psychology and sociology to study habits, norms, and non-economic behavior.[^wzth9i]

## Other senses

### 1. Academic field family

Behavioral sciences can mean the family of empirical disciplines studying behavior and the mental processes behind it.[^64j9h8]

- This usage includes psychology, ethology, behavioral economics, behavioral genetics, and parts of anthropology and sociology.[^64j9h8]
- The disciplines are connected less by one subject matter than by a shared commitment to explaining behavior through evidence, observation, and experimentation.[^64j9h8]
- In innovation work, this academic sense supplies the theories and methods behind customer research, experimentation, organizational diagnosis, and adoption studies.[^s52vys][^wzth9i]

### 2. Behavioral economics

Behavioral economics is a branch of behavioral science concerned specifically with economic decisions such as saving, purchasing, and choosing among offers.[^wzth9i]

- George Katona developed an influential mid-century program joining psychology and economics through surveys and a theory of the “economic mind.”[^jl2yz0]
- In startup practice, behavioral economics is useful for pricing, incentives, defaults, risk perception, and decisions made under uncertainty.[^bglzc6][^wzth9i]
- It should not be used as a synonym for every study of human behavior; research on team norms, leadership, identity, or workplace habits may belong to behavioral science without being behavioral economics.[^wzth9i]

# Etymology and Origin

- The plural term gained prominence in mid-twentieth-century American science as a neutral umbrella for psychologists, sociologists, anthropologists, and biologists pursuing empirical explanations of behavior.[^64j9h8]
- George Katona’s University of Michigan work from 1946 to 1981 represents an important earlier effort to unite psychology and economics around observed economic behavior.[^jl2yz0]
- The term later migrated into business and innovation practice as organizations began applying behavioral research to customer decisions, technology adoption, management, and change implementation.[^s52vys][^39vtxt][^fuy2cw]

# Adjacent Vocabulary

- **Synonyms**:
  - **Behavioral science**: the common singular form; often used in consulting and product practice for the same broad field.[^wzth9i]
  - **Behavioral research**: emphasizes the research process—observing, measuring, and testing behavior—rather than the broader academic disciplines.[^64j9h8][^wzth9i]
  - **Human factors**: narrower and more design-oriented, usually focused on how people interact with tools, systems, and environments.
  - **Behavioral economics**: a related but narrower branch focused on economic decisions.[^wzth9i]

- **Antonyms**:
  - **Intuition-led decision-making**: choosing based primarily on judgment or instinct rather than behavioral evidence.
  - **Stated-preference research**: relying on what people report they would do, without validating actual behavior.
  - **Technological determinism**: assuming technology itself will produce adoption or change regardless of social, organizational, and behavioral conditions.[^s52vys]

# Usage in Practice

- Annie Riley describes management as “applied behavioral science,” because managers are ultimately trying to influence customers and coach teams toward observable actions.[^39vtxt]
- The Decision Lab summarizes the practical distinction as: “Behavioral science asks why people act as they do and tests what will change it.”[^wzth9i]
- In adoption research, the behavioral question is whether people move “from exposure to use, from use to routine dependence,” and sometimes from dependence to rejection.[^s52vys]
- A behavioral approach to change management “reframes the job as redesigning choice environments” so desired behaviors occur more reliably.[^fuy2cw]
- Behavioral science is most useful when “people know what to do and still do not do it,” or when a team must measure how much an intervention changes behavior.[^wzth9i]
- In applied research, candidate interventions are tested against a control group to determine whether a change shifts actual behavior at scale.[^wzth9i]

# Common Misuses

- Calling any customer survey “behavioral science.” **Better term:** [[Market Research]], unless the work explains behavior and tests an intervention against observed outcomes.[^wzth9i]
- Calling a persuasive feature “behavioral science” without a behavioral hypothesis, measurable outcome, or experiment. **Better term:** [[Product Design]] or [[Growth Marketing]].
- Treating behavioral science as synonymous with behavioral economics. **Better term:** [[Behavioral Economics]] when the subject is specifically pricing, purchasing, saving, incentives, or other economic decisions.[^wzth9i]
- Using “behavior change” to mean communication alone. **Better term:** [[Change Management]] when the work concerns implementation, processes, incentives, and sustained organizational adoption.[^fuy2cw][^wzth9i]
