# Defining and Describing Price Discrimination

![A SaaS pricing page showing basic, pro, and enterprise tiers with different features and prices](https://ecdn.teacherspayteachers.com/cdn-cgi/image/format=avif,quality=70,onerror=redirect/thumbitem/Price-Discrimination-First-Second-Third-Degree-Price-Discrimination-2724058-1500873602/750f-2724058-1.jpg)

- _Price discrimination is a pricing practice in which a startup or other seller charges different customers different prices for the same, or substantially similar, offering based on differences in willingness to pay rather than differences in cost._ [^ms87q1] [^jgod8q]
- In innovation consulting, the term applies when a company uses customer segmentation, product tiers, usage levels, geography, or eligibility rules to capture more of the value different users place on an offering. [^ms87q1] [^jgod8q] It does not mean that every price difference is discriminatory: prices may legitimately differ because delivery costs, features, service levels, or contractual obligations differ. [^ms87q1] [^jgod8q] Consultants care because pricing can determine adoption, conversion, revenue capture, and whether a product can serve both budget-constrained users and high-value enterprise customers. [^jgod8q]

## Disambiguation

### Primary sense — the innovation-consulting sense

**Price discrimination is the deliberate design of prices or purchasing menus so that customer segments with different willingness to pay contribute different amounts for an otherwise comparable product or service.** [^ms87q1] [^jgod8q]

- **First-degree discrimination** attempts to charge each customer close to that customer’s maximum willingness to pay; in practice, startups usually approximate this through negotiated sales, individualized offers, or data-informed pricing rather than achieving “perfect” discrimination. [^7s1idb] [^ms87q1]
- **Second-degree discrimination** lets customers sort themselves through quantity discounts, usage bands, feature tiers, bundles, or basic/pro/enterprise versions. [^7s1idb] [^jgod8q]
- **Third-degree discrimination** charges identifiable groups different prices, such as students, nonprofits, regions, or customer segments with different demand elasticities. [^7s1idb] [^ms87q1]
- The strategy normally requires some combination of market power, information about customer types, and limits on arbitrage—preventing a low-price customer from reselling to a high-price customer. [^eqa33b] [^jgod8q]
- It is **not automatically price discrimination** when a higher-priced plan includes materially greater functionality, support, capacity, or service obligations; that may instead be ordinary product differentiation or cost-based pricing. [^ms87q1] [^jgod8q]

## Other senses

### 1. Economic-theory classification

Price discrimination is the microeconomic analysis of how a seller allocates prices across buyers or buyer groups to extract consumer surplus and increase revenue or profit. [^lrg8iv] [^eqa33b]

- Arthur Pigou’s framework distinguishes first-, second-, and third-degree discrimination. [^lrg8iv] [^7s1idb]
- First-degree pricing targets an individual’s reservation price; second-degree pricing varies with quantity or version; third-degree pricing varies across identifiable groups. [^7s1idb] [^ms87q1]
- The framework is useful for founders because it converts a vague question—“Who will pay more?”—into testable decisions about segmentation, self-selection, packaging, and resale risk. [^jgod8q]

### 2. Algorithmic or personalized pricing

In contemporary technology markets, price discrimination can also refer to using customer data, observed behavior, or digital identity to personalize prices or offers. [^7s1idb]

- Personalized pricing is a technologically enabled approximation of first-degree discrimination, although firms often personalize discounts, promotions, or recommendations rather than publish a unique public price for every user. [^7s1idb]
- This practice raises governance and trust questions: customers may perceive opaque or identity-based price differences as unfair even when the strategy increases revenue.
- For an innovation team, the relevant design problem is not only whether personalization improves monetization, but whether customers understand and accept the basis for different offers.

### 3. Public-policy and welfare analysis

Price discrimination is also used to evaluate effects on consumer surplus, producer surplus, access, and market fairness. [^7s1idb] [^ms87q1]

- Perfect discrimination can transfer nearly all consumer surplus to the seller, while some forms of segmentation can expand output or make a service available to lower-income users. [^7s1idb]
- The same mechanism can therefore support inclusion—such as discounted access for students or nonprofits—or produce exclusion when high prices are targeted at groups with fewer alternatives.
- Regulatory treatment depends on the market, jurisdiction, conduct, and rationale; the label alone does not establish that a pricing practice is unlawful.

## Etymology and Origin

- The modern economic treatment is generally associated with English economist **Arthur Cecil Pigou**, who formalized the concept in *The Economics of Welfare*, published in 1920. [^lrg8iv] [^7s1idb]
- Pigou’s framework classified the practice into three degrees: individualized pricing, self-selection through quantities or versions, and group-based pricing. [^lrg8iv] [^7s1idb]
- Later economists, including Joan Robinson, George Stigler, and Paul Samuelson, developed the analysis in work on monopoly, competition, and market structure. [^lrg8iv]
- The concept migrated naturally into startup and innovation vocabulary as software made versioning, usage measurement, customer segmentation, and negotiated enterprise pricing easier to implement. [^7s1idb] [^jgod8q]

## Adjacent Vocabulary

- **Synonyms**:
  - **Differential pricing**: a broad, often more neutral term for charging different customers different prices.
  - **Segmented pricing**: emphasizes identifiable customer groups or market segments.
  - **Personalized pricing**: emphasizes individual-level data and offers.
  - **Value-based pricing**: prices according to perceived customer value; it may produce price discrimination, but does not require different prices for otherwise similar customers.

- **Antonyms**:
  - **Uniform pricing**: one posted price for all comparable customers.
  - **Cost-plus pricing**: price derived primarily from production or delivery cost plus a markup.
  - **Price parity**: maintaining the same price across specified channels or markets.

- **Adjacent terms**: [[Willingness to Pay]], [[Value-Based Pricing]], [[concepts/Market Segmentation|Market Segmentation]], [[Product-Led Growth]], [[SaaS Pricing]], [[Versioning]]

## Usage in Practice

- “Price discrimination is the practice of charging different prices to different customers.” [^ms87q1]
- Pigou’s first-degree case charges each consumer their “maximum willingness to pay.” [^7s1idb]
- In second-degree discrimination, “each consumer faces the same price schedule,” with different prices for different quantities. [^bf897v]
- Third-degree discrimination charges different prices to “broad, observable groups,” such as students or seniors. [^7s1idb]
- A common technology example is a menu in which “basic/pro/enterprise software pricing” lets buyers self-select. [^jgod8q]
- Effective discrimination depends on “market power,” the ability to identify buyer types, and the prevention of arbitrage. [^jgod8q]

## Common Misuses

- **Calling every tiered product price discrimination.** If each tier delivers materially different features, capacity, or support, **product differentiation** or **versioning** is more precise. [^ms87q1] [^jgod8q]
- **Calling a cost-based price difference discrimination.** If one customer costs more to serve because of logistics, implementation, support, or compliance requirements, use **cost-to-serve pricing** rather than price discrimination. [^ms87q1] [^jgod8q]
- **Calling a discount merely “personalized pricing.”** A discount based on a public promotion, coupon, or customer self-selection is usually **promotional pricing** or **second-degree discrimination**, not necessarily individualized pricing. [^7s1idb] [^ms87q1]
- **Using “price discrimination” as a synonym for unfairness or illegality.** The economically precise term describes differentiated pricing; the appropriate alternatives may be **deceptive pricing**, **unfair discrimination**, or **predatory pricing**, depending on the conduct and jurisdiction.
