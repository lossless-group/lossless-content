In the Biography of [[Steve Jobs]], [[organizations/Apple|Apple]]'s first investor and then first head of marketing introduced the core principle of Impute. The idea was to get people to buy, marketers need to induce a set of feelings that make them want to buy.  

Thus, marketing is about influencing how people move from perception to feeling. Impute is the opposite of compute. The human mind does not rationally compute all the necessary factors, they perceive many factors all at once, often many that they could not rationally point out and determine, much less influence the decision to purchase.

# Defining and Describing Impute Marketing


_Impute marketing is the practice of shaping what a product’s presentation causes people to assume about its quality before they experience it._

The expression adapts **“Impute,”** one of three principles in Mike Markkula’s 1977 *Apple Marketing Philosophy*, alongside empathy and focus. [^7rlz7k] In this sense, *impute* means to attribute qualities or causes to something; marketing applies that idea to the judgments people make from packaging, design, copy, advertising, and other signals. [^oelyj0] The concept is most useful when a product’s perceived credibility, quality, or status may diverge from its underlying performance. [^z0rgmr]

## Uses in Context

- **Brand and visual identity:** Marketers use the concept to argue that design, packaging, and other visible details signal product quality before direct use. [^7rlz7k]
- **Landing-page critique:** A page may be evaluated not only for conversion copy but for “what it’s imputing about your brand before anyone reads a word.” [^z0rgmr]
- **Performance marketing:** The concept frames weak results as a possible presentation problem rather than only a targeting, attribution, or media-buying problem. [^z0rgmr]
- **Advertising and creative strategy:** A campaign can be judged by whether its execution makes the product appear worth noticing before a viewer clicks or investigates. [^45629m]
- **Product launches:** The idea explains why polished presentation can establish trust and expectations before customers have tested the product. [^7rlz7k]

## History of Use

## Origins

The documented origin of the marketing sense is Mike Markkula’s January 1977 one-page *The Apple Marketing Philosophy*, which identified empathy, focus, and impute as its three principles. [^7rlz7k] The source describes impute as the idea that people judge a product by its presentation and that quality must be signaled through packaging, design, and every customer impression. [^7rlz7k] Available search results do not establish that Markkula coined the standalone phrase **“Impute Marketing”**; they instead show the phrase being used retrospectively to name a marketing application of his principle. [^z0rgmr] [^45629m] [^xofu9q]

## Evolution

- **1977 — Product philosophy:** Markkula’s formulation positioned impute as a general marketing principle: presentation influences judgments about product quality. [^7rlz7k]
- **2025–2026 — Performance-marketing reinterpretation:** Contemporary practitioners connected impute to conversion performance, arguing that execution can cause performance numbers to reflect presentation quality rather than product quality. [^z0rgmr] [^xofu9q]
- **2026 — Broader brand-signaling usage:** The term was applied to landing pages, pitches, billboards, and other pre-experience signals intended to make an offering appear worth attention. [^45629m] [^gbz6yx]

## Case Studies

**Apple’s presentation system.** In 1977, Mike Markkula’s *Apple Marketing Philosophy* defined impute alongside empathy and focus. [^7rlz7k] Its central premise was that customers judge quality through presentation, including packaging, design, and other impressions. [^7rlz7k] Later descriptions connect the principle with Apple’s white boxes, retail stores, and keynote presentations. [^7rlz7k] The case shows impute marketing operating as a coordinated system: individual design decisions reinforce a common inference about quality rather than functioning as decoration alone. [^7rlz7k]

**FullEnrich’s minimalist billboard example.** A 2026 practitioner account described placing FullEnrich on a Times Square billboard with three words and no call to action, characterizing the choice as “pure impute.” [^45629m] The intended effect was not to explain the product in detail but to make it feel sufficiently notable to deserve attention before an audience clicked or learned more. [^45629m] This illustrates how impute marketing can prioritize status, memorability, and perceived importance over immediate information density. [^45629m]

**The performance-marketing reframing.** Recent practitioner commentary argues that marketers often focus on CTR, CAC, and ROAS while overlooking the quality signals embedded in execution. [^z0rgmr] The argument is that a strong product can underperform when its landing page, advertising, or presentation makes it appear less capable than it is. [^z0rgmr] A related account says impute helps determine whether performance numbers reflect actual product quality or merely its presentation. [^xofu9q] This case reframes impute from a brand-design principle into a diagnostic lens for explaining weak conversion performance. [^z0rgmr] [^xofu9q]
