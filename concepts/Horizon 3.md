# Defining and Describing Horizon 3

![Three Horizons framework showing Horizon 1, Horizon 2, and Horizon 3 across time and uncertainty](https://cdn.wccftech.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Forza-Horizon-3-02-Splashing-Around.jpg)

_Horizon 3 is the deliberately protected space where organizations explore uncertain, radical possibilities before they become mainstream businesses._

In the Three Horizons framework, **Horizon 3** denotes long-term, uncertain opportunities that may eventually replace or reshape an organization’s current activities.[8] It commonly includes research projects, pilots, experiments, minority investments, and exploratory ventures rather than mature revenue lines.[4] The concept matters because it separates future-oriented discovery from the operational demands of the present, allowing organizations to pursue transformation without judging early experiments by Horizon 1 performance standards.[1][4]

# Uses in Context

- **Corporate strategy:** Horizon 3 describes “options on future growth,” including research projects, pilot programs, and minority stakes in new businesses.[4]
- **Innovation portfolios:** Strategy teams use it to classify experiments that are too immature or uncertain to compete directly with established businesses.[1][4]
- **Futures and systems thinking:** The framework uses Horizon 3 for “radical breaks, paradigm shifts, and fundamentally new systems.”[8]
- **Public-sector and social innovation:** Health and social-care practitioners use Horizon 3 to articulate aspirational, person-centred future systems rather than incremental service improvements.[7]
- **Technology-market analysis:** Industry analysts sometimes use “Horizon 3” as a maturity or market-position label for ecosystem-level transformation, including agentic services.[9]
- **Supply-chain transformation:** Consulting frameworks apply the term to autonomous or connected supply networks built around data-powered ecosystems and co-innovation.[5]

# History of Use

## Origins

The modern business-strategy version of Horizon 3 is associated with Mehrdad Baghai, Stephen Coley, and David White’s 2000 work on the Three Horizons model.[1] The framework emerged from research into high-growth companies and the need to manage current businesses, emerging opportunities, and longer-term options concurrently.[4] Its intellectual roots also extend into futures thinking, organizational foresight, and educational innovation rather than originating solely as a technology-company concept.[10]

## Evolution

- **1996:** An early Three Horizons formulation used Disney to distinguish immediate improvements, rebuilding of existing businesses, and exploratory moves such as the first Disney Store and new media investments.[4]
- **2000:** Baghai, Coley, and White formalized the model as a portfolio approach to managing growth across multiple time horizons.[1]
- **2010s–2020s:** The framework expanded beyond corporate growth into public services, education, healthcare, supply chains, and systems transformation.[5][7][10]
- **Contemporary use:** Some practitioners describe Horizon 3 as extending roughly 10–30 or more years into the future, emphasizing deep uncertainty and paradigm change.[8]

# Best Real-World Examples

- [Airbnb](https://www.airbnb.com) — repurposed existing internet and peer-to-peer technologies into a disruptive lodging model, an example of rapid Horizon 3 commercialization.[1][4]
- [Uber](https://www.uber.com) — combined mobile connectivity, location data, and flexible labor into a new transportation model that challenged incumbent taxi systems.[1][4]
- [SpaceX](https://www.spacex.com) — pursued a long-term transformation of launch economics through reusable-rocketry research and development.[1]
- [Tesla](https://www.tesla.com) — helped move electric vehicles from an alternative technology toward a mass-market automotive strategy.[1][4]
- [Craigslist](https://www.craigslist.org) — illustrates how a comparatively simple digital platform could disrupt established classified-advertising models.[1][4]
- [Nuka System of Care](https://www.scfhealth.org/) — serves as a third-horizon exemplar for a fully person-centred health-and-care system.[7]
- [Nova Innovation](https://novainnovation.com) — used European collaborative funding and tidal-energy development to expand a future-oriented clean-energy system.[6]

# Case Studies

**Airbnb and Uber: platform models emerging from existing technologies.** Airbnb and Uber are frequently cited as Horizon 3 examples because they did not require entirely new foundational technologies; instead, they recombined internet platforms, mobile devices, location services, and distributed participants into new business models.[1][4] Their significance lies in the speed with which a seemingly peripheral model could challenge established industries. The cases show that Horizon 3 innovation is not necessarily defined by laboratory invention: it can arise from a novel configuration of available technologies, assets, and user behavior.[1][4]

**Fife health and social care: Horizon 3 as a future system rather than a product.** In Scotland’s Fife SHINE work, participants used the Three Horizons framework to explore a shift from crisis-driven services toward a humanizing, person-centred system organized around the question “What matters to you?”[7] Horizon 3 clarified the desired long-term system while the other horizons helped participants distinguish current constraints and transitional practices.[7] This case demonstrates that Horizon 3 can guide institutional and social transformation, not merely corporate product development.

**Tidal energy and Nova Innovation: exploratory technology supported by collaboration.** Nova Innovation coordinated the Enabling Future Arrays in Tidal project, which brought together companies, universities, and European innovation organizations to improve tidal-energy technology and reduce costs.[6] European funding enabled the company to expand its tidal array, including a reported doubling of its size.[6] The example shows how Horizon 3 efforts often depend on coalitions, public funding, research institutions, and staged experimentation rather than on a single incumbent’s internal innovation program.[6]
