# Snapshot

_*Datacenter Operations* is the operating layer for keeping physical computing infrastructure available, efficient, observable, and increasingly optimized for AI workloads—from power and cooling through servers, networks, facilities, capacity, and software control. The category is expanding from conventional data-center management into “AI-factory” orchestration, where electricity, thermal headroom, workload scheduling, and GPU utilization are managed as one system._

> “The data center market has seen explosive funding activity over the last five quarters, with $28.5 billion raised across just nine deals.”[2]

This profile captures the category as of **October 2026**, with emphasis on the convergence of data-center infrastructure, facility operations, AI infrastructure, power, cooling, and operational software. It is worth maintaining as a reference card because AI demand is shifting the center of gravity from software-only infrastructure toward scarce physical capacity, while capital is concentrating in operators that can secure power, deploy high-density facilities, and deliver compute at hyperscale.

# What is this Market Category?

Datacenter Operations covers the products, services, and operating companies that keep data-center facilities functioning: facility monitoring, infrastructure management, power distribution, cooling, environmental controls, capacity planning, asset management, maintenance, security, remote hands, workload-aware optimization, and the operation of colocation or hyperscale campuses. The customer set includes cloud providers, hyperscalers, enterprises, colocation operators, AI-compute providers, telecommunications companies, governments, and financial institutions.

The software layer commonly includes data-center infrastructure management, or DCIM; computerized maintenance management; building-management integrations; observability; energy-management systems; workload orchestration; and automation. The physical layer includes electrical systems, generators, UPS equipment, switchgear, cooling systems, racks, cabling, sensors, robotics, and high-density liquid-cooling infrastructure.

The category includes both **operators** that own or run facilities and **enablers** that sell equipment or software into those facilities. It excludes ordinary enterprise IT operations that do not materially manage physical data-center assets, general cloud application monitoring, and pure data-center construction or real-estate development unless those activities include ongoing operational control.

The boundary is disputed at the edges: some operators treat power procurement, data-center construction, and campus development as separate infrastructure markets, while AI-infrastructure companies increasingly present power, cooling, workload scheduling, and GPU capacity as one integrated “AI factory” operating system.[5][13]

# Why Now?

- **AI workloads have made physical constraints economically decisive.** High-density GPU clusters increase the importance of power availability, thermal management, rack density, and workload-aware operations; the market is therefore moving beyond traditional uptime monitoring toward optimization of output per watt.[7][15]

- **Capital has shifted toward integrated AI-factory capacity.** The funding data shows large rounds for companies that combine data-center construction, power access, and AI-compute operations rather than selling isolated infrastructure components; Crusoe, Nscale, Applied Digital, and Aligned are examples of this concentration.[2][3]

- **Power and cooling have become bottlenecks rather than back-office concerns.** New entrants are targeting microfluidic cooling, modular deployment, power-rich campuses, and energy-linked sites, indicating that operations increasingly begins with the physical design of the facility and its energy system.[1][5]

- **Automation is moving upstream into design, construction, and ongoing management.** Y Combinator has sought startups applying software, machine learning, and robotics to site selection, construction, setup, supply-chain management, and ongoing data-center operation.[13]

- **Investors are treating data centers as infrastructure-plus-compute platforms.** KKR’s participation in a reported $50 billion joint venture for AI-oriented data centers and power infrastructure illustrates the scale at which financial capital is combining facilities, energy assets, and AI demand.[10]

# What’s Happening?

## CAGR and TAM

- A market estimate cited in the available search results places **data-center facility management at approximately $200 billion in 2024**, reaching **$400 billion by 2033** at an estimated **8.5% CAGR for 2026–2033**; the framing includes infrastructure management, network management, colocation, server infrastructure, storage, and operations and maintenance.[15] This is a broad top-down category estimate rather than a narrowly defined DCIM-software market.

- The available results do not provide a directly verifiable Gartner, IDC, Forrester, or MarketsandMarkets primary report with a comparable Datacenter Operations definition. That limits apples-to-apples comparison: the cited $200 billion estimate appears to include facility and infrastructure services, while narrower DCIM or data-center-management software estimates would produce materially smaller TAMs.

- Funding momentum is a more observable near-term proxy than category revenue. One tracked dataset reports **$28.5 billion across nine data-center-market deals** over five quarters, with **$13.1 billion raised in Q1 2025**, largely because Aligned Data Centers accounted for approximately 92% of that quarter’s total.[2]

## Category creation events

- The category is crystallizing around the term **AI factory**: Phaidra describes software agents that coordinate power, cooling, and workload management to maximize computational output per watt, turning operations from passive facility monitoring into active optimization.[7]

- **Crusoe’s capital formation** is a defining category event. The company reportedly raised $1.375 billion in a 2025 Series E to expand AI data-center infrastructure, with the round associated with a valuation above $10 billion in secondary coverage.[3][9]

- The category is also being created through the convergence of operators and energy infrastructure. Funding activity includes power-rich Texas campuses, Nordic AI/HPC facilities, modular AI data centers, and high-density cooling systems, suggesting that the reference implementation is no longer a conventional enterprise data center but an AI-optimized campus.[1][3][5]

## Capital concentration

- Capital has concentrated in **campus-scale operators and AI-compute platforms**. Reported transactions include approximately $12 billion for Aligned Data Centers in Q1 2025, $1.38 billion for Crusoe, $1.1 billion for Nscale, and up to $900 million for Applied Digital.[2][3]

- **Crusoe** is one of the clearest capital-aggregation examples: coverage reports approximately $14.6 billion across six disclosed funding events involving the company, including equity, credit facilities, and project financing.[2]

- The next capital pool is forming around **enabling infrastructure**. Corintis raised a reported $24 million Series A for in-chip microfluidic cooling, while Phaidra has raised approximately $50 million for AI-driven data-center operations optimization.[1][7]

# Frontier and Open Questions

- **Will Datacenter Operations remain a facilities-management category, or become an AI-factory control plane?** Phaidra and other Innovators are pushing toward software that coordinates power, cooling, and workloads, while Incumbents such as Microsoft, Google, Schneider Electric, and Vertiv control the installed infrastructure and operational data.[7]

- **Does power procurement belong inside the category?** Crusoe, Nscale, Applied Digital, and other Challengers treat energy access and campus development as inseparable from compute operations, while traditional DCIM vendors generally begin at the facility-management layer.[1][2]

- **Can liquid cooling become a standard operating requirement for AI facilities?** Corintis and other cooling innovators are betting that chip-level or direct-to-chip cooling is necessary for future density, while incumbent thermal-management vendors are extending existing facility architectures.[1]

- **Will operators build their own automation or buy a neutral control layer?** Hyperscalers can integrate operations into proprietary platforms, whereas independent companies such as Phaidra, Future Facilities, and specialized DCIM vendors are attempting to become cross-facility systems of record.[7]

- **Will AI-compute challengers become durable infrastructure operators or finance-dependent capacity aggregators?** Crusoe, CoreWeave, Nscale, Lambda, and Applied Digital have attracted unusually large financing packages, but the available funding evidence combines equity, debt, credit facilities, and project finance, making comparisons of underlying operating economics difficult.[2][3][4]

- **Does the category include construction and design automation?** Y Combinator’s interest in software and robotics for site selection, construction, setup, and ongoing management suggests an expansive boundary, while conventional operators may classify those activities as development or engineering rather than operations.[13]

# Adjacent Concepts and Categories

- **AI Factories** — the emerging operating model in which power, cooling, compute, and workload orchestration are designed as one integrated production system.
- **Data Center Infrastructure Management (DCIM)** — the software foundation for monitoring assets, capacity, energy, environmental conditions, and facility performance.
- **GPU Cloud** — the compute-consumption layer operated by companies such as CoreWeave, Lambda, Crusoe, and Nscale.
- **Liquid Cooling** — the thermal-management architecture increasingly required by high-density AI systems.
- **Power Infrastructure** — generation, transmission, substations, UPS systems, batteries, and energy procurement that determine usable data-center capacity.
- **Digital Twins** — simulation models used to test facility layouts, airflow, cooling, capacity, and operational changes before deployment.
- **Hyperscale Data Centers** — very large facilities operated for cloud, internet, and AI workloads, with automation and standardization at campus scale.
- **Workload Orchestration** — software that schedules and places compute according to GPU availability, power constraints, cooling limits, latency, and customer demand.
