# Electrical Power Monitoring Systems

Electrical Power Monitoring Systems (EPMS) have emerged as a distinct, rapidly professionalizing layer in the broader energy and infrastructure software stack, sitting between traditional building management systems and utility-scale energy management platforms to provide continuous, high-resolution visibility into electrical conditions, power quality, and asset performance across facilities and data centers. ## What is this Market Category?

Electrical Power Monitoring Systems can be defined as integrated solutions—combining meters, sensors, communication networks, software, and visualization tools—that continuously measure, collect, and analyze electrical parameters across low‑ and medium‑voltage distribution systems within facilities, campuses, and data centers to improve power availability, reliability, efficiency, and safety. [^2refk3] [^3o7h2v] [^sq3jus] [^3p4mej] [^yisp6p] [^tp6rbm] [^lbli9o] [^mg0ld0] Schneider Electric describes its EPMS software, EcoStruxure Power Monitoring Expert, as a system designed to interrogate devices such as wireless PowerTag sensors and power quality instruments, collect energy and power information across the local network, and transform it into actionable information via web applications for energy and power management. [^2refk3] Eaton’s Foreseer EPMS and Brightlayer EPMS are framed similarly: vendor‑neutral platforms that connect “an operation’s vast array of devices, regardless of the manufacturer or model,” provide real‑time power and environmental monitoring across single or multiple sites, and deliver high‑resolution trend analysis, forecasting, and power quality analytics. [^3p4mej] [^yisp6p] [^lbli9o] ABB’s energy and power monitoring solutions deploy sensors, analytics, and AI to connect physical infrastructure and motor‑driven systems, monitor energy use, identify waste, and adjust energy flows through distributed control systems, again emphasizing continuous monitoring and optimization across the energy lifecycle from generation to consumption. [^31r8no] [^0mq4q6]  

The problems EPMS products solve cluster into four main domains. First, they address power reliability and uptime by monitoring the state of electrical distribution networks, detecting faults, and enabling fast isolation or load shedding to avoid outages and equipment damage. [^2refk3] [^31r8no] [^vh6xk4] [^ndh6a5] [^0mq4q6] [^ttmh62] [^3p4mej] [^yisp6p] [^tp6rbm] [^lbli9o] [^mg0ld0] ABB’s Relion protection solutions and SCADA systems, for example, are used to detect and isolate faults and maintain efficient power delivery, while distributed control systems like ABB Ability System 800xA can shed non‑critical loads within milliseconds to keep critical operations running. [^31r8no] [^0mq4q6] Second, EPMS tackles energy efficiency and cost management by tracking consumption across circuits and loads, enabling energy managers to evaluate energy usage, track performance, and identify opportunities for savings. [^2refk3] [^31r8no] [^sq3jus] [^3p4mej] [^yisp6p] [^e8h5x4] [^tp6rbm] [^vp0zvh] [^lbli9o] [^mg0ld0] Schneider Electric explicitly presents EcoStruxure Power Monitoring Expert as a tool to help energy managers evaluate energy usage, manage energy costs, and support sustainability initiatives, while ABB Ability energy manager focuses on optimizing energy consumption and CO2 footprint with data‑driven insights. [^31r8no] [^sq3jus] Third, EPMS addresses power quality and electrical asset health by monitoring voltage, current, harmonics, frequency, and other quality indicators, identifying sources of disturbances, and supporting proactive maintenance. [^2refk3] [^3o7h2v] [^ndh6a5] [^ttmh62] [^3p4mej] [^yisp6p] [^tp6rbm] [^lbli9o] [^mg0ld0] ABB, for instance, highlights its power quality monitoring solutions and EMAX2 breakers with integrable power control modules as means to detect harmonics, avoid equipment failures, and manage loads during peak hours. [^ndh6a5] [^ttmh62] Fourth, EPMS supports compliance and safety by providing detailed electrical data that aids verification of installations and supports standards‑aligned monitoring of protective measures, as reflected in IEC 61557‑12’s requirements for power metering and monitoring devices (PMD). [^3o7h2v] [^vh6xk4] [^3p4mej] [^yisp6p]  

The category is sold primarily to operators of power‑critical or energy‑intensive facilities—data centers, industrial plants, large commercial buildings, campuses, hospitals, and infrastructure installations—as well as to utilities and energy service companies that manage distributed assets for industrial or commercial customers. [^2refk3] [^31r8no] [^0mq4q6] [^sq3jus] [^ttmh62] [^3p4mej] [^yisp6p] [^tp6rbm] [^k3pg4q] [^lbli9o] [^mg0ld0] Data center operators adopting DCIM platforms with integrated EPMS capabilities seek centralized visibility into power, cooling, environmental conditions, and capacity to manage increasingly complex, high‑density infrastructure. [^k4w5xo] [^k3pg4q] [^lbli9o] [^mg0ld0] Building owners and facility managers deploy building energy management systems (BEMS) and EPMS together to continuously monitor power consumption of electricity, gas, and water, with BEMS focusing on cross‑vector energy use and EPMS deepening electrical insights. [^vp0zvh] [^yisp6p] Industrial operators and utilities adopt ABB’s distributed control systems, SCADA, and power quality solutions to integrate renewables, monitor performance of turbines and motors, and optimize plant operations. [^31r8no] [^vh6xk4] [^0mq4q6] [^ttmh62]  

Importantly, the EPMS boundary excludes several adjacent technologies that a naive reader might assume are part of the category. Electricity metering equipment used for revenue metering, governed by standards such as IEC 62053, is explicitly out of scope in IEC 61557‑12’s definition of PMD, even though PMD devices may reuse uncertainty definitions from energy metering standards. [^3o7h2v] Power quality instruments (PQI) defined under IEC 62586, while often deployed alongside EPMS solutions and sometimes integrated into EPMS dashboards, are treated as a separate instrument category with distinct performance requirements and are not themselves EPMS software. [^2refk3] [^3o7h2v] [^ndh6a5] Likewise, utility‑scale energy management systems (EMS) used by grid operators to monitor, control, and optimize generation and transmission systems are defined in Allied Market Research’s EMS report as tools for operators of electric utility grids, which operate at different voltage levels and system boundaries than facility‑level EPMS. [^e8h5x4] Generic building management systems (BMS) that focus on HVAC, lighting, and general building automation without deep electrical monitoring are also outside the strict EPMS category, even though Eaton’s white paper on “Building Management Systems vs Electrical Power Monitoring Systems” emphasizes that combining BMS and EPMS can be advantageous for some facilities. [^yisp6p]  

Where the boundary is fuzzy and contested is in the overlap between EPMS, DCIM, and BEMS, especially as vendors converge traditionally separate software solutions into unified digital platforms that monitor IT assets, facility infrastructure, power, cooling, and environmental conditions. [^k4w5xo] [^k3pg4q] [^lbli9o] [^mg0ld0] Eaton’s Brightlayer data centers suite explicitly “breaks the traditional barriers of data center infrastructure management (DCIM), electrical power monitoring system (EPMS) and distributed IT management” by converging asset management, IT and OT device monitoring and control, power quality metrics, IT automation, and advanced electrical supervision into a single application, raising legitimate questions about whether EPMS should be defined narrowly as electrical‑only monitoring or more broadly as the electrical spine within such converged platforms. [^lbli9o]  

## Why Now?

### Digitalization, IoT Sensors, and Data‑Driven Power Management

One of the central forces making Electrical Power Monitoring Systems a coherent and expanding category is the maturation of digitalization and IoT sensor networks for electrical infrastructure. Schneider Electric highlights that “the power digitalization process is based on the collection of energy usage, power quality, and electrical asset performance data,” enabled by devices such as PowerTag wireless energy sensors that measure consumption and expose measurements via communication protocols across local networks. [^2refk3] These sensors feed EPMS software like EcoStruxure Power Monitoring Expert, which is “specifically designed to interrogate these types of devices across the local area network and collect energy and power information,” then process and present it as actionable information via web applications. [^2refk3] Grand View Research similarly notes that addressing modern electrical challenges in power management “requires IoT‑enabled sensors, electrical meters, smart breakers, and other intelligent equipment” to continuously monitor electrical conditions, with digital technologies combined with power management systems enhancing efficiency, reducing downtime, and enabling faster decision‑making. [^tp6rbm] Eaton’s Brightlayer EPMS, described as a digital platform that delivers real‑time and historical visibility into electrical power systems, exemplifies how sensor data, high‑resolution logging, and waveform analysis now form a unified digital backbone for electrical supervision. [^lbli9o]  

ABB’s narrative reinforces this digitalization force, emphasizing that “through a combination of sensors, analytics and AI, we connect physical infrastructure and electrical and motor‑driven systems to monitor energy use, pinpoint areas of energy waste and provide recommendations on how to reduce energy consumption.” [^31r8no] Distributed control systems such as ABB Ability System 800xA constantly monitor power demands in real time and adjust energy flows accordingly, ensuring power is available when and where needed. [^31r8no] [^0mq4q6] ABB’s Relion protection and low‑voltage digital units, along with SCADA and condition‑monitoring systems for wind turbines, form part of an increasingly instrumented electrical environment in which continuous monitoring is both technically feasible and expected. [^31r8no] [^vh6xk4] [^0mq4q6] The convergence of sensing, connectivity, and analytics capabilities across these incumbent portfolios has lowered the floor for EPMS implementations, making it viable to deploy monitoring at scale across circuits, feeders, and devices that previously remained “dumb” or minimally metered. [^2refk3] [^31r8no] [^vh6xk4] [^0mq4q6] [^tp6rbm] [^lbli9o]  

Eaton’s S&P‑branded white paper on digitalization, electrification, and data centers further quantifies this momentum, reporting that “energy/power monitoring is widely deployed (53%), while sustainability tracking is deployed or planned by most (41% deployed; 45% planned), as is predictive AI/ML monitoring of IT and power/cooling (27% deployed; 45% planned).” [^mg0ld0] The same survey notes that deployment momentum is especially strong around electrical‑focused digital platforms: EPMS upgrades have been deployed by 39% of organizations, with 48% planning deployment, and EPMS broadly shows 30% deployed and 49% planned when looking at electrical‑focused digital platforms. [^mg0ld0] These figures situate EPMS in a broader digitalization arc where data‑driven monitoring is becoming a default expectation rather than an optional upgrade.  

### Energy Costs, Sustainability Pressure, and CO₂ Accountability

A second force is the rising pressure on organizations to manage energy costs and meet sustainability and decarbonization targets, which pushes EPMS from a nice‑to‑have reliability tool into a central instrument of ESG strategy. Allied Market Research characterizes energy management systems as computer‑aided tools used by operators of electric utility grids to monitor, control, and optimize generation and transmission performance, and values the global EMS market at USD 36.2 billion in 2020, projecting it to reach USD 161.9 billion by 2030 at a 16.2% CAGR from 2021 to 2030. [^e8h5x4] While EMS, as defined here, is focused on utility‑scale operations, the same economic and sustainability drivers—need to reduce energy costs, improve efficiency, and integrate renewables—cascade down to facility‑level EPMS deployments. [^2refk3] [^31r8no] [^0mq4q6] [^tp6rbm] [^lbli9o] [^mg0ld0] ABB’s Ability energy manager, for instance, explicitly “monitors and optimizes energy consumption and CO2 footprint, enabling faster and better decisions based on data insights,” positioning EPMS solutions as tools for carbon accounting and optimization. [^31r8no]  

Building Energy Management Systems (BEMS), which Allied Market Research values at USD 6.5 billion in 2022 and projects to reach USD 18.5 billion by 2032 at an 11.2% CAGR, are described as centralized platforms employing sensors, meters, and control algorithms to gather real‑time facts on a building’s energy usage across electricity, gas, and water. [^vp0zvh] One of the foremost purposes of BEMS is “continuous monitoring and reporting of power consumption,” indicating that electrical monitoring is foundational to building‑level energy management. [^vp0zvh] Eaton’s white paper on EPMS and Brightlayer goes further, arguing that advanced EPMS helps data centers understand their use of water, air, gas, electricity, and steam (WAGES) to reduce operating costs and meet sustainability goals, thereby integrating EPMS into broader resource efficiency strategies. [^lbli9o]  

The Eaton S&P survey data underscores that ESG is “becoming operational,” with 25% of organizations tracking ESG metrics and nearly half prioritizing ESG outcomes as part of operational planning. [^mg0ld0] As electric dependence grows, the white paper argues that “predictive monitoring, power quality analytics and AI-based modeling are critical for managing volatility and sequencing investments,” implicitly relating these capabilities to both economic and environmental performance. [^mg0ld0] Together, these sources suggest that EPMS is increasingly being justified not only on reliability grounds but also on its ability to support cost reduction, sustainability reporting, and regulatory compliance around energy use and emissions. ### Data Center Complexity, DCIM Convergence, and Centralized Visibility

A third force is the dramatic increase in data center complexity and power density, which has driven the rise of Data Center Infrastructure Management (DCIM) as a category and, within it, strengthened demand for integrated power monitoring, EPMS, and electrical supervision. Schneider Electric’s EcoStruxure IT Expert illustrates this convergence by offering a cloud‑based, vendor‑agnostic DCIM solution that delivers “wherever-you-go monitoring and visibility into your distributed IT physical infrastructure,” including real‑time insight into power, cooling, and IT assets with smart alarms, AI‑powered insights, and built‑in sustainability and UPS health assessments. [^k4w5xo] EcoStruxure IT Expert is framed as centralizing data center infrastructure management, with integration to EcoStruxure Power Monitoring Expert for power‑critical and energy‑intensive facilities, thereby linking DCIM, EPMS, and power automation in a single ecosystem. [^lbli9o]  

Eaton’s S&P white paper emphasizes that deployment momentum is strongest “around electrical-focused digital platforms: EPMS (30% deployed; 49% planned) and AI energy analytics (23% deployed; 48% planned) are moving quickly,” while sustainability tracking and predictive AI/ML monitoring of IT and power/cooling also show strong deployment and planning numbers. [^mg0ld0] This suggests that as data centers adopt DCIM to manage complexity, EPMS functionality—real‑time power monitoring, power quality analytics, event analysis—is increasingly inseparable from DCIM buying decisions. [^k4w5xo] [^k3pg4q] [^lbli9o] [^mg0ld0] The result is a category dynamic in which EPMS is both a standalone market for industrial and building applications and a key subsystem within DCIM platforms, contributing to centralized visibility and performance optimization across digital infrastructure. ### Standards, Safety Expectations, and Power Quality Awareness

A fourth enabling condition is the codification of standards and safety expectations around electrical monitoring, which legitimizes EPMS as a formal component of safe and high‑performance power systems. IEC 61557‑12:2018 specifies requirements for power metering and monitoring devices (PMD) that measure and monitor electrical quantities within distribution systems up to 1 000 V AC and 1 500 V DC. [^3o7h2v] The standard defines performance for single‑ and three‑phase AC or DC systems, covering fixed or portable devices intended for indoor and outdoor use, and emphasizes that PMD devices provide additional safety information that aids verification of installations and enhances distribution system performance. [^3o7h2v] While IEC 61557‑12 does not address functional safety or cybersecurity, it clearly distinguishes PMD from electricity metering equipment for revenue purposes and from power quality instruments conforming to IEC 62586, thereby carving out a formal instrument category directly aligned with EPMS deployments. [^3o7h2v]  

ABB’s messaging around power quality monitoring and management shows how awareness of power quality has become mainstream. Its power quality monitoring solution is presented as providing faster and more accurate information for decision‑making, enabling quick problem identification and solutions to reduce maintenance costs and improve reliability, while monitoring and controlling power networks to avoid outages, equipment damage, failures, and interruptions of critical operations. [^ndh6a5] [^ttmh62] ABB highlights that full monitoring of power quality and power reliability allows easy detection of where harmonics are created in data centers and other facilities, underscoring the link between quality monitoring and uptime. [^ndh6a5]  

ABB’s low‑voltage monitoring and control portfolio, which includes single‑phase and three‑phase voltage monitoring relays, temperature monitoring, insulation monitoring, and grid feeding monitoring relays for renewable energy and distributed generation, reflects a broader safety culture in which continuous monitoring of voltages, currents, temperatures, insulation resistance, and grid synchronization is expected for critical equipment. [^vh6xk4] ABB’s CM‑UFD grid feeding monitoring relays, for instance, ensure proper synchronization with the grid, check power quality issues, and prevent islanding in renewable applications. [^vh6xk4] This standard‑driven and safety‑oriented environment encourages organizations to adopt EPMS not only as an efficiency tool but as an integral part of electrical safety and compliance infrastructure. [^3o7h2v] [^vh6xk4] [^ndh6a5] [^ttmh62] [^3p4mej] [^yisp6p] [^tp6rbm] [^lbli9o] [^mg0ld0]  

### Renewables Integration, Grid Volatility, and Load Flexibility

A final force is the growing penetration of renewables and distributed energy resources, which introduces volatility into power systems and requires more sophisticated monitoring and control at the facility level. ABB emphasizes that its solutions integrate distributed energy sources, like wind and solar, with battery storage to manage power flows and optimize electricity use within local grids, using sensors, analytics, and AI to connect infrastructure and motor‑driven systems. [^31r8no] ABB’s distributed control systems, including System 800xA, play a critical role in ensuring reliable power supply in industrial facilities by constantly monitoring power demands and adjusting energy flows, shedding non‑critical loads within milliseconds to keep critical operations running while increasing the share of renewables in the energy mix. These capabilities point to a world in which facilities increasingly operate as microgrids with on‑site generation, storage, and flexible loads, making EPMS a core infrastructure for managing power flows, load shedding, and quality in the presence of variable renewable input. ### Category Creation Events: Product Launches, Standards, and Convergence Platforms

Several defining events and product launches have helped crystallize Electrical Power Monitoring Systems as a recognizable category, even if the term EPMS itself is more prominent in practitioner whitepapers than in major analyst taxonomies. One such marker is the publication of IEC 61557‑12:2018, which formalized requirements for power metering and monitoring devices (PMD) used in low‑voltage distribution systems. [^3o7h2v] By defining PMD performance, scope, and exclusions—explicitly distinguishing PMD from revenue metering equipment and from power quality instruments—the standard gave manufacturers and operators a clear reference point for devices intended to support EPMS‑style monitoring. [^3o7h2v] While the standard does not address functional safety or cybersecurity, its focus on additional safety information and enhanced distribution system performance reinforced the legitimacy of continuous electrical monitoring as part of safe installation practice. [^3o7h2v]  

ABB’s Customer World 2018 event in Xiamen, where the company showcased a series of power quality monitoring and management products covering all voltage levels and supporting customers in utilities, industry, transport, and infrastructure to improve power quality management and ensure reliable and economical grid operation, can be seen as another category‑shaping moment. [^ttmh62] ABB’s presentation of an internet‑based energy management system, eEMS Studio, capable of monitoring energy‑consuming equipment, providing protection and fault monitoring, managing operations, and collecting and analyzing energy consumption data across buildings, industry, transport, data centers, and manufacturing, exemplified how EPMS capabilities were being framed as integrated, cross‑sector solutions. [^ttmh62] The EMAX2 air circuit breaker, described as enabling total power management by protecting electric circuits and reducing energy consumption based on user demands, with an integratable power control module to evaluate consumption and manage loads during peak hours, further reinforced the integration of monitoring, protection, and control in EPMS architectures. [^ttmh62]  

Eaton’s Foreseer EPMS and related technical whitepapers have played a significant role in naming EPMS as a distinct category. Foreseer is explicitly described as an “electrical power monitoring system (EPMS)” that connects an operation’s vast array of devices regardless of manufacturer or model, facilitates real‑time power and environmental monitoring across single or multiple locations worldwide, and helps organizations reduce power consumption costs and avoid unplanned downtime. [^3p4mej] [^yisp6p] Eaton’s technical documentation emphasizes core features such as comprehensive monitoring to improve power management, extensive device‑driven library interfaces, energy reporting for facility efficiency, and scalable vendor‑neutral monitoring across thousands of devices and multiple facilities. [^yisp6p] The inclusion of a white paper titled “Building Management Systems vs Electrical Power Monitoring Systems: Is a combination the right solution for your facility?” explicitly positions EPMS as a distinct category requiring strategic consideration alongside BMS. [^yisp6p]  

More recently, Eaton’s Brightlayer data centers suite white paper frames EPMS as part of a converged digital platform that “breaks the traditional barriers of data center infrastructure management (DCIM), electrical power monitoring system (EPMS) and distributed IT management,” introducing Brightlayer EPMS software as a module delivering real‑time and historical visibility into electrical power systems, maximizing equipment uptime, and enabling comprehensive power quality and waveform analysis. [^lbli9o] That Brightlayer EPMS is named as such and positioned alongside DCIM modules suggests a maturation of the EPMS label within data center discourse. [^lbli9o] [^mg0ld0]

## Frontier and Open Questions

Several frontier questions shape the evolution of Electrical Power Monitoring Systems and will likely be influenced by incumbents, challengers, and innovative platforms in different ways. One key question is whether EPMS will converge completely with DCIM and building energy management systems into unified digital platforms, or whether it will retain a distinct identity as a specialized electrical monitoring layer. Eaton’s Brightlayer and Schneider’s EcoStruxure architecture push toward convergence, integrating EPMS with DCIM and BEMS, while standards like IEC 61557‑12 focus on electrical‑specific devices, preserving a distinct technical identity. [^2refk3] [^3o7h2v] [^k4w5xo] [^yisp6p] [^k3pg4q] [^lbli9o] [^mg0ld0] Incumbents and DCIM challengers are most likely to drive this convergence or maintain modular boundaries through their platform designs.  

A second question concerns how far AI and predictive analytics will penetrate EPMS, transforming it from descriptive monitoring into prescriptive and autonomous control. ABB, Eaton, and Schneider already reference AI‑enabled analytics and predictive monitoring, and Eaton’s survey shows substantial deployment and planning for predictive AI/ML monitoring of IT and power/cooling. [^31r8no] [^0mq4q6] [^lbli9o] [^mg0ld0] The frontier lies in using EPMS data for real‑time optimization, anomaly detection, and automated remediation, potentially enabling self‑healing power systems. Incumbent platforms like ABB Ability, Brightlayer, and EcoStruxure IT Expert, along with DCIM challengers like Sunbird and Nlyte, are poised to shape this trajectory.  

A third open question is how cybersecurity and functional safety will be integrated into EPMS standards and implementations. IEC 61557‑12 explicitly does not address cybersecurity or functional safety aspects, leaving a gap that becomes more critical as EPMS devices and software connect to networks and cloud platforms. [^3o7h2v] [^t8h6hc] [^k4w5xo] [^tp6rbm] [^lbli9o] [^mg0ld0] As EPMS becomes central to power‑critical facilities and data centers, protecting EPMS data and control pathways from cyber threats becomes as important as ensuring electrical safety. Incumbent vendors with strong security practices and DCIM challengers with cloud‑native architectures will need to define best practices, and standards organizations may extend PMD definitions to incorporate security requirements.  

A fourth question relates to EPMS’s role in decentralization and microgrid management. ABB’s renewables integration narratives and grid feeding monitoring relays point to a future where facilities act as microgrids with on‑site generation, storage, and flexible loads. [^31r8no] [^vh6xk4] [^0mq4q6] [^ttmh62] EPMS could evolve into the nerve system of these microgrids, coordinating power flows, managing islanding, and supporting market participation in demand response or local energy trading. Incumbents like ABB and Eaton, with strong positions in distributed control systems and EPMS, will likely lead here, but new innovators could emerge with specialized microgrid EPMS platforms.  

A fifth question is whether EPMS data will become systematically integrated into ESG reporting and regulatory compliance frameworks, moving beyond internal optimization into external disclosure. BEMS and EMS reports already highlight continuous monitoring, reporting, and sustainability tracking, and Eaton’s survey indicates that ESG metrics tracking is becoming operational. [^e8h5x4] [^vp0zvh] [^lbli9o] [^mg0ld0] EPMS data—power quality, uptime, energy efficiency, emissions impacts—could become part of standardized ESG reports or regulatory filings. Incumbent platforms with sustainability dashboards and AI‑powered insights, such as EcoStruxure IT Expert and ABB Ability energy manager, are likely to drive this integration.  

Finally, there is a category‑boundary dispute around whether EPMS should encompass utility‑scale EMS and grid‑side monitoring or remain focused on facility and data center applications. Allied’s EMS definition is clearly grid‑operator‑centric, [^e8h5x4] while EPMS discussions in Eaton and Schneider materials center on facilities. [^2refk3] [^3p4mej] [^yisp6p] [^lbli9o] Some operators may view EPMS as part of a continuum from building to grid, especially in microgrid contexts, blurring boundaries. How analysts and operators choose to define EPMS over the next few years—narrowly electrical‑facility or broadly energy‑system—will shape market sizing, vendor strategies, and investment theses.
