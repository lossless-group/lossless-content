# Snapshot

_**Mobility-as-a-Service (MaaS)** is the orchestration layer that lets travelers plan, book, pay for, and use multiple transport modes through one digital experience. The category is expanding from trip-planning apps into an operating model for integrated public transit, shared mobility, taxis, micromobility, parking, and—at its most ambitious edge—autonomous transport._

> “The global Mobility-as-a-Service market was valued at USD 244.3 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach USD 1,354.0 billion by 2035, representing an 18.7% CAGR.” [^ryjsy1]

This profile captures the category as of the current research window, while recognizing that market reports use radically different definitions: one report estimates a 2025 market of USD 3.2 billion and a 32% CAGR, whereas broader reports count the gross transaction value of underlying transport services and estimate hundreds of billions of dollars. [^jc4gfi] [^ryjsy1] The reference card is therefore most useful as a strategic category map, not as a single authoritative revenue forecast.

# What is this Market Category?

Mobility-as-a-Service combines mobility supply, journey planning, reservations, ticketing, payments, identity, and—sometimes—subscription or bundled pricing into a single customer journey. Its buyers and economic stakeholders include transit agencies, municipalities, transport operators, employers, universities, airports, property owners, and consumers. The core product is not a vehicle; it is the coordination layer that helps a user select and transact across public transport, ride-hailing, taxis, car sharing, bike sharing, scooters, parking, rail, ferries, and demand-responsive transport.

The category includes multimodal journey planners, integrated ticketing platforms, mobility wallets, MaaS operating systems for cities, transit-as-a-service platforms, transport aggregation APIs, and consumer applications that combine several modes. It can also include corporate mobility platforms when they aggregate commuting, parking, transit benefits, ride-hailing, and micromobility.

The category excludes ordinary single-mode ride-hailing, standalone fleet-management software, vehicle telematics, basic navigation apps, and mobility hardware unless those products participate in a broader multimodal orchestration or payment layer. A ride-hailing app is adjacent to MaaS; it becomes a MaaS product when it is integrated with other transport modes and used as part of a coordinated journey.

The boundary is disputed at two edges: narrow MaaS definitions restrict the category to public-private multimodal integration and account-based ticketing, while broader commercial definitions count nearly every digitally mediated transport transaction—including car sharing, ride-hailing, parking, and sometimes autonomous mobility—as MaaS. [^jc4gfi] [^ryjsy1]

# Why Now?

- **Digital payments and account-based ticketing lowered the integration barrier.** MaaS products can now abstract away from proprietary paper tickets and fragmented operator back offices by connecting identity, payment, entitlement, and trip data in one account. This is the technological condition that turns “find a route” into “buy and complete a journey.”

- **Urban transport has become a portfolio problem rather than a single-mode problem.** Cities and employers increasingly need to combine rail, buses, shared cars, bicycles, scooters, taxis, and demand-responsive services to address congestion, emissions, and first/last-mile gaps. The resulting demand favors orchestration platforms over isolated mobility apps.

- **The underlying transaction pool is large enough to support category formation.** Depending on methodology, 2025 global MaaS estimates range from USD 244.3 billion to USD 532.76 billion, with forecast CAGRs from 18.7% to 18.75% through the mid-2030s. [^jm9aas] [^ryjsy1] The spread itself is evidence that the category is coalescing around different definitions of the same strategic opportunity.

- **Public-sector digitization is expanding the addressable buyer set.** MaaS is increasingly sold not only to consumers but also to transit authorities, municipalities, airports, campuses, and employers—customers that can subsidize adoption, mandate interoperability, or provide privileged access to transport data.

## Category creation events

- The most important category-creation event is the movement from standalone journey planning toward integrated transaction infrastructure: the market is increasingly defined by the ability to plan, book, pay, and access multiple modes rather than merely compare routes.

- Market-report divergence has itself become a category signal. Reports now separately model public and private MaaS segments, regional adoption, car sharing, transaction value, and platform revenue, indicating that “MaaS” is being used both as a software category and as a broader mobility-economy umbrella. [^p5m6ey] [^jc4gfi]

- The category is coalescing around a two-sided operating model: transit agencies and cities provide access to regulated or publicly supported mobility supply, while technology companies aggregate that supply with private operators and monetize transactions, software, subscriptions, or platform services.

- The available search results do not provide a reliable, current cross-market total for MaaS venture funding or a verifiable ranking of lead investors. Funding should therefore be tracked company by company rather than inferred from the market-size figures above.

# Frontier and Open Questions

- **Will MaaS be a consumer super-app or public-sector infrastructure layer?** Incumbents such as Google and Uber favor consumer distribution, while challengers such as Via and Masabi are better positioned to resolve the public-agency and transaction-infrastructure side.

- **Who owns the customer relationship when a journey spans regulated and private transport?** Transit agencies, payment providers, mapping platforms, and ride-hailing companies all have plausible claims; the winner may depend more on procurement and account-based ticketing than on app quality.

- **Can multimodal bundles achieve superior economics to pay-as-you-go mobility?** Whim-style subscription concepts test whether customers value predictable access across modes, while operators must decide whether bundles increase utilization or erode per-trip margin.

- **Should ride-hailing and car sharing count as MaaS, or only as integrated supply components?** Broad market reports count private mobility transactions, while narrow software reports treat MaaS as the orchestration layer; this definitional dispute materially changes TAM estimates. [^jm9aas] [^jc4gfi] [^ryjsy1]

- **Will autonomous vehicles expand MaaS or displace multimodal transport?** Incumbent vehicle, mapping, and ride-hailing platforms are likely to control the supply layer, while innovators may compete on dispatch, fleet orchestration, and public-transit integration.

- **Can open standards prevent platform lock-in?** Transit agencies and cities have an incentive to preserve interoperable data and ticketing systems, while large platforms benefit from proprietary identity, payment, and demand data.

# Adjacent Concepts and Categories

- **TransitTech** — The software and infrastructure layer for public transport operations, fares, scheduling, and passenger information.

- **Ride-Hailing** — A major private mobility supply mode that becomes part of MaaS when integrated into multimodal planning or payment.

- **Micromobility** — Bikes, scooters, and other lightweight vehicles that solve first- and last-mile transport gaps.

- **Account-Based Ticketing** — The payment and entitlement model that allows travelers to access transit through an account rather than a mode-specific paper or stored-value ticket.

- **Mobility Wallets** — Digital accounts that combine payment, subsidies, benefits, tickets, and mobility entitlements.

- **Demand-Responsive Transit** — Flexible shared transport coordinated through software rather than fixed routes and schedules.

- **Smart Cities** — The broader municipal technology context in which MaaS connects transport, curb management, data, payments, and urban planning.

- **Autonomous Mobility** — Driverless vehicles and fleet systems that could become a future supply layer inside MaaS.
