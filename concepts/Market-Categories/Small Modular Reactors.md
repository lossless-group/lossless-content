# Snapshot

_Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) are factory-oriented nuclear power systems designed to deliver reliable electricity and heat in smaller, potentially repeatable units than conventional gigawatt-scale reactors. The category is moving from technology demonstration toward commercialisation, with artificial-intelligence data-center demand, government support, and improving licensing readiness concentrating capital around a small set of developers._

> “The global small modular reactor market size was valued at **USD 6.5 billion in 2025** and is projected to reach **USD 10.7 billion by 2033**, representing a **6.8% CAGR** from 2026 to 2033.”[1]

This profile captures the category as of **October 2026**, including reactor vendors, fuel and component platforms, project developers, and publicly traded SMR specialists. It is worth maintaining as a reference card because the category now sits at the intersection of nuclear decarbonisation, industrial heat, energy security, and the rapidly increasing electricity requirements of hyperscale computing.

# What is this Market Category?

Small Modular Reactors are nuclear fission power systems designed around smaller individual reactor units, modular construction, and—depending on the design—factory fabrication and serial deployment. The category includes light-water SMRs, high-temperature gas reactors, sodium-cooled fast reactors, molten-salt concepts, and other advanced designs that generally target smaller output per module than conventional large reactors.

SMR products are sold primarily to utilities, governments, industrial companies, mining operators, district-heating providers, and data-center or large-load customers seeking firm, low-carbon energy. Their value proposition is not simply “smaller nuclear”: developers are attempting to reduce construction risk, allow incremental capacity additions, serve locations that cannot absorb a gigawatt-scale plant, and provide electricity or heat for industrial processes.

The category includes reactor design, licensing, nuclear fuel, reactor modules, balance-of-plant systems, project development, construction, operation, and long-term service. It also includes enabling technologies such as advanced fuels, passive safety systems, thermal storage, and factory-manufacturing infrastructure when those technologies are specifically tied to SMR deployment.

The boundary excludes conventional large reactors merely because they are being built in modular sections, as well as microreactors whose output and deployment model are materially smaller than mainstream SMRs. It also excludes generic nuclear services, uranium mining, grid-scale batteries, and renewable generation unless they are directly integrated into an SMR project.

The boundary is disputed at the edges: some developers and analysts include microreactors, floating reactors, and nuclear heat systems, while others reserve “SMR” for grid-connected reactors with standardized modules and output typically in the tens to hundreds of megawatts. The OECD Nuclear Energy Agency’s dashboard illustrates the breadth of the field by tracking **127 SMR technologies**, of which 74 were featured in its 2025 edition.[8]

# Why Now?

- **AI and data-center electricity demand created a new anchor customer.** Financial coverage reported that SMR developers raised at least **$1.5 billion** during the year to February 2025, with the fundraising linked to power-supply agreements involving Big Tech; X-energy’s $700 million financing included technology and institutional investors.[12] This matters because data centers can value 24/7 low-carbon power more highly than conventional wholesale buyers.

- **Government support reduced first-of-a-kind project risk.** TerraPower’s Natrium project is associated with a US Department of Energy award of approximately **$2 billion**, while X-energy has received substantial US government support connected to its advanced reactor and TRISO-X fuel platform.[13][15] Public funding is functioning as a bridge across the period in which private developers must finance licensing, fuel qualification, and first deployments before serial manufacturing benefits appear.

- **Licensing and deployment readiness improved across multiple designs.** The [[OECD]] Nuclear Energy Agency’s SMR Dashboard evaluates not only technical feasibility but also licensing, siting, financing, supply chain, stakeholder engagement, and fuel readiness.[8] Its expansion from 56 assessed reactors in the 2024 edition to 127 tracked technologies in the 2025 edition indicates a broader and more mature development pipeline, even though most designs remain pre-commercial.

- **The category gained a visible public-market financing channel.** Oklo, NuScale, and Nano Nuclear were reported to have raised more than **$700 million** through share sales and other financing mechanisms over the preceding year.[12] Public listings have increased investor visibility for SMRs, but they have also made the category more sensitive to execution risk, dilution, licensing timelines, and the distinction between a reactor design and a contracted operating asset.

- **Modular manufacturing became a central commercial thesis.** Developers increasingly frame SMRs as products that can be standardized, manufactured, and deployed in fleets rather than as bespoke civil megaprojects. This thesis is strongest for designs with repeatable modules and a stable fuel supply, but it remains unproven at large commercial scale because first-of-a-kind projects still face licensing, supply-chain, construction, and financing bottlenecks.[8]

## Category creation events

- The OECD Nuclear Energy Agency’s SMR Dashboard has become one of the category’s most useful institutional tracking systems, moving beyond technical feasibility to assess licensing, siting, financing, supply chain, engagement, and fuel readiness.[8] That methodology helps convert a large set of reactor concepts into a comparable commercial-readiness framework.

- The emergence of publicly traded specialist developers crystallized SMRs as an investable category rather than solely a government nuclear-technology program. Oklo, NuScale, and Nano Nuclear were identified as public-market companies that collectively raised more than $700 million through share sales and other financing mechanisms in the year preceding February 2025.[12]

- Big Tech participation transformed the category narrative from long-horizon decarbonisation to near-term power procurement. X-energy’s $500 million Series C-1 financing was anchored by Amazon in 2024, while later coverage described a broader financing wave connected to data-center electricity demand.[14]

## Capital concentration

- Capital has concentrated heavily in a small number of developers. Data cited by Business Insider from CB Insights and PitchBook indicated that four companies received approximately **92% of nearly $3.2 billion** in equity funding directed to SMR-focused firms over the prior eight years.[14]

- X-energy has been one of the clearest beneficiaries of that concentration. The company announced a **$500 million Series C-1** financing anchored by Amazon in 2024, and later reporting described a **$700 million** financing involving Jane Street and other institutional investors.[12][14]

- Public-market capital has flowed toward a narrow group of listed names. Oklo, NuScale, and Nano Nuclear together raised more than $700 million through public share sales and related financing mechanisms over the relevant twelve-month period, while private developers such as TerraPower, X-energy, Rolls-Royce SMR, Holtec, and GE Hitachi continued to pursue projects and strategic capital.[12]

# Frontier and Open Questions

- **Can SMRs achieve repeatable factory economics rather than merely smaller megaproject economics?** Incumbents such as Westinghouse, GE Hitachi, and Rolls-Royce SMR are best positioned to resolve the manufacturing and supply-chain question because they possess industrial procurement and nuclear-quality systems, while challengers must prove that their designs can be replicated without first-of-a-kind cost overruns.[8]

- **Will data centers become the first durable commercial customer segment for SMRs?** Amazon’s investment in X-energy and the broader financing wave tied to AI electricity demand suggest strong early pull, but utilities and regulators will determine whether data-center contracts can support bankable nuclear projects at scale.[12][14]

- **Which fuel platforms become the commercial bottleneck: conventional enriched uranium, high-assay low-enriched uranium, or advanced fuels such as TRISO?** X-energy’s integrated TRISO-X strategy makes fuel availability a core competitive issue, while the NEA explicitly treats fuel readiness as a separate commercialization dimension.[8][13]

- **Will advanced reactors outperform certified light-water designs, or will licensing familiarity win?** NuScale’s NRC-certified design has a regulatory lead, whereas TerraPower, X-energy, Kairos, and other challengers pursue different coolants, fuels, and safety architectures that may offer better economics but face greater licensing complexity.[13]

- **Should microreactors be included in the SMR category?** Nano Nuclear and Radiant Industries sit near the lower-output boundary, and including them expands the category toward remote power, military installations, and industrial loads; excluding them keeps SMR analysis focused on grid-scale or utility-scale modules.[12]

- **Can public-market enthusiasm survive long development cycles and uncertain project economics?** Oklo, NuScale, and Nano Nuclear have increased visibility and financing access, but the category’s public companies remain exposed to dilution, licensing delays, construction risk, and the gap between a reactor design announcement and operating revenue.[12]

# Adjacent Concepts and Categories

- **Advanced Nuclear** — the broader technology category containing SMRs, microreactors, advanced fuels, and non-light-water reactor designs.

- **Microreactors** — smaller nuclear systems that border SMRs and serve remote, military, industrial, and highly distributed loads.

- **Nuclear Fuel Cycle** — the mining, enrichment, fabrication, transport, recycling, and waste-management infrastructure required to operate SMR fleets.

- **HALEU Fuel** — high-assay low-enriched uranium required by several advanced reactor designs and a major commercialization constraint.

- **Industrial Process Heat** — a key non-electricity application for high-temperature gas, molten-salt, and other advanced reactors.

- **Data-Center Power** — the emerging demand category linking AI infrastructure growth with firm low-carbon electricity procurement.

- **Nuclear Licensing and Siting** — the regulatory and local-acceptance processes that often determine whether a technically viable reactor can become a commercial project.

- **Thermal Energy Storage** — an enabling technology for designs such as Natrium that combine nuclear heat with flexible electricity output.
