# Snapshot

_Integrated Electrical and Mechanical Systems is the physical infrastructure layer that makes AI-oriented data centers operable: power distribution, generation, cooling, controls, and the engineering/construction work that integrates them into a functioning facility. The category is expanding because AI rack density is turning electrical capacity and heat rejection—not compute silicon alone—into the binding constraints on data-center deployment._

> “The Data Center Construction Market size is expected to grow from USD 281.34 billion in 2025 to USD 431.39 billion by 2031, at a 7.51% CAGR.”[1]

This profile captures the category as of October 2026, using data-center construction and power/cooling infrastructure as the closest measurable market proxies. It is worth maintaining as a reference card because the market is coalescing around a buyer problem—delivering high-density compute quickly and reliably—rather than around a single product class.

# What is this Market Category?

Integrated Electrical and Mechanical Systems comprises the engineered hardware, software, and delivery services that connect a data center’s compute load to power and thermal infrastructure. The electrical side includes utility interconnection, substations, transformers, switchgear, UPS systems, busways, generators, batteries, power distribution, and controls; the mechanical side includes chillers, cooling towers, pumps, heat exchangers, liquid-cooling distribution, direct-to-chip systems, immersion systems, and associated building services.[1][2]

The category is sold primarily to hyperscalers, colocation providers, AI-cloud operators, enterprises building private AI capacity, utilities, general contractors, and data-center developers. Its commercial value lies in integrating these systems into a safe, maintainable facility with predictable power quality, thermal performance, commissioning, and uptime—not merely selling an individual component.[1][4]

The category excludes semiconductors, servers, accelerators, model software, and ordinary building construction that does not materially affect data-center power or thermal performance. It also excludes standalone utility generation and transmission unless they are developed specifically as part of the data-center capacity solution; the boundary is therefore broader than “data-center equipment” but narrower than the entire energy and construction ecosystem.

The boundary is disputed at the edges: some operators include on-site generation, grid interconnection, and project finance because “energizing” capacity is inseparable from the facility, while others restrict the category to equipment and EPC integration inside the data-center fence line.[4]

# Why Now?

- **AI rack density has made cooling a first-order design constraint.** In the United States, average rack power is reported to be rising from 8 kilowatts in 2024 to 15 kilowatts in 2026, while mechanical infrastructure is forecast to grow at a 9.87% CAGR—faster than the broader electrical segment.[2]

- **Capital is shifting toward the physical “energizers” of compute.** McKinsey estimates that roughly $1.3 trillion of the projected $7 trillion data-center investment race will flow to power generation and transmission, cooling, and electrical equipment.[4]

- **Electrical capacity is becoming the gating item for AI expansion.** Electrical systems represented 39.95% of 2025 data-center construction budgets globally, making power infrastructure the largest measurable component of the construction stack.[1]

- **Liquid cooling is moving from specialist technology to mainstream infrastructure.** Funding tracked across cooling-focused companies—including ZutaCore, Omen AI, Orbital Industries, and Barocal—reached $191 million in the cited 2026 period, indicating that thermal management is attracting dedicated infrastructure capital rather than being treated only as an ancillary server feature.[6]

- **The buyer is increasingly purchasing an integrated delivery outcome.** McKinsey’s framing of “builders” and “energizers” reflects a market in which land, construction, grid capacity, cooling, and electrical equipment must be coordinated rather than procured as isolated work packages.[4]

# What's Happening?

## CAGR and TAM

- **Mordor Intelligence, “Data Center Construction Market Size & Growth 2031.”** The global market is estimated at $281.34 billion in 2025 and $300.38 billion in 2026, reaching $431.39 billion by 2031 at a 7.51% CAGR for 2026–2031; the report segments the market by infrastructure category and identifies electrical systems as 39.95% of 2025 budgets.[1]

- **Fortune Business Insights, “Data Center Construction Market Share, Size, Trend, 2034.”** This estimate places the 2025 global market at $275.16 billion and forecasts $761.19 billion by 2034, implying an 11.97% CAGR; the materially higher result illustrates the lack of a single market definition and likely reflects different inclusion rules, forecast windows, and market-model assumptions.[3]

- **Global Market Insights, “Data Center Construction Market Size & Share 2026–2035.”** The firm estimates a narrower $227.6 billion market in 2025 and forecasts $434.7 billion by 2035 at a 6.8% CAGR, further demonstrating that “data-center construction” is a proxy rather than a standardized category for integrated electrical and mechanical systems.[10]

## Category creation events

- **AI infrastructure has reframed data-center construction as an “energizing” problem.** McKinsey’s 2025 report explicitly separates builders from energizers and assigns approximately 25% of the $7 trillion investment opportunity to generation, transmission, cooling, and electrical equipment.[4]

- **Mechanical systems are becoming a distinct growth segment rather than a secondary construction line item.** Mordor reports that mechanical systems are growing at 9.31% globally through 2031, despite electrical systems holding the largest 2025 budget share.[1]

- **Direct-to-chip and two-phase cooling have become reference architectures for high-density AI facilities.** The funding record cited for Accelsius describes two-phase direct-to-chip cooling for gigawatt-class facilities, while ZutaCore is described as providing waterless two-phase direct-to-chip infrastructure for AI and HPC data centers.[7]

## Capital concentration

- **Funding is concentrated in physical-capacity providers.** A September 2026 funding analysis reports $4.07 billion across 12 disclosed data-center financings, with four financings representing approximately 83% of the total.[6]

- **The largest pools of capital are going to operators and developers, not component startups.** A separate deal compilation reports more than $35 billion across 45 data-center funding deals from 2024–2026 and says operators generally raised hundreds of millions or billions, while infrastructure-technology rounds were usually below $150 million.[12]

- **Cooling capital is clustering around direct-to-chip and immersion approaches.** The cited funding analysis lists LiquidStack’s $20 million Series B, Submer’s $55.5 million Series C, Accelsius’s $24 million Series A and $65 million Series B, and ZutaCore’s $100 million Series C.[7]

# Frontier and Open Questions

- **Will “integrated systems” include generation and grid interconnection, or stop at the data-center fence line?** Incumbents such as Caterpillar, Eaton, Schneider Electric, utilities, and large EPC firms are most likely to resolve this through bundled project delivery, while McKinsey’s “energizer” framing supports the broader boundary.[4]

- **Will liquid cooling become the default architecture for AI facilities, or remain a premium layer for only the densest racks?** Challengers such as LiquidStack, Submer, Accelsius, and ZutaCore are positioned to answer this through deployments, reliability data, and total-cost-of-ownership evidence.[7]

- **Can the electrical supply chain scale as quickly as AI demand?** Incumbents with transformer, switchgear, UPS, generator, and controls capacity are advantaged, but early-stage companies such as Claros and Amperesand are targeting alternative power-delivery models.[11][12]

- **Will customers buy complete “capacity outcomes” instead of separate equipment packages?** Hyperscalers, colocation providers, and challengers such as QTS, Vantage, and Aligned will determine whether integrated EPC and infrastructure-as-a-service contracts displace fragmented procurement.

- **Do market reports measure the same market?** The reported 2025 global market ranges from $227.6 billion to $281.34 billion, while forecast CAGRs range from 6.8% to 11.97%; analysts must normalize geography, inclusion rules, and whether the estimate covers construction, EPC, equipment, or total facility investment.[1][3][10]

- **Can waterless or two-phase systems overcome operational conservatism?** ZutaCore and Accelsius are advancing waterless and two-phase architectures, while Johnson Controls and Legrand provide incumbent channels that may accelerate adoption or preserve conventional hybrid designs.[7]
