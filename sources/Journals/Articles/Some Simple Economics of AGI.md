## Why It Matters to Innovators

- **Verification becomes the scarce resource.** The paper argues that the cost of generating plausible outputs is falling, while the cost of determining whether those outputs are correct remains tied to human time, expertise, evidence, and real-world feedback. [^4t6yug] This shifts strategic attention from raw model capability to  ([[Agent Accreditation]]) infrastructure.
- **The “Measurability Gap” explains why capability does not equal adoption.** The authors define a widening distance between what AI can economically execute and what people can economically verify. [^238w7q] [^4t6yug] This is a useful diagnostic for products that automate high-stakes, ambiguous, or slow-feedback workflows.
- **Automation boundaries should be framed as measurable versus non-measurable.** Rather than relying only on the conventional distinction between routine and non-routine work, the paper argues that tasks become economically automatable when their outputs can be measured reliably. [^238w7q] This connects directly to [[concepts/Jobs-to-be-Done|Jobs-to-be-Done]] and [[concepts/Explainers for AI/Agentic Workflows|Agentic Workflows]].
- **Agentic scaling can be capped by oversight capacity.** As compute expands the frontier of what agents can execute, society’s ability to oversee and verify those actions may become the binding constraint on realized economic value. [^238w7q] For innovators, this makes audit trails, evaluation systems, human escalation, and outcome instrumentation part of the core product—not compliance afterthoughts.
- **The paper installs a practical investment heuristic:** prioritize markets where verification is cheap, frequent, objective, and digitally observable; be cautious where correctness emerges only after long delays or depends on tacit expertise. [^238w7q] [^4t6yug] This helps identify strong candidates for [[Vocabulary/Workflow Automations|Workflow Automations]] and distinguish them from superficially attractive but operationally unverifiable use cases.
