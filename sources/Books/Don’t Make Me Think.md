# Don’t Make Me Think

![Cover of Steve Krug’s *Don’t Make Me Think, Revisited*](https://sensible.com/divi/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/DMMT-cover-262x300.png)

- _A compact, practical book that turns web usability into a common-sense discipline: make interfaces obvious, reduce cognitive friction, and test with real people._
- **Don’t Make Me Think** is a **book** by usability consultant Steve Krug, first published by Que in 2000.[3] Its central argument is that websites and digital products should be self-evident rather than requiring users to stop and interpret them.[3] Consultants return to it because it provides a durable diagnostic lens for identifying avoidable friction in customer experiences.

## The People Behind It

- **Steve Krug** is a usability consultant whose career has included more than 20 years of work with clients including Apple, Bloomberg.com, Lexus.com, NPR, and the International Monetary Fund.[9]
- He is also the author of *Rocket Surgery Made Easy*, a companion book focused on practical, do-it-yourself usability testing.[9]
- Krug’s distinctive contribution is translating user-experience principles into accessible rules and repeatable practices for designers, product teams, and executives rather than treating usability as a specialist-only concern.[1][9]

## Catalog of Notable Works

- **“Don’t Make Me Think!”** — The governing principle: users should not need to pause to decode a page, interface, or navigation system.[3]
- **“How We Really Use the Web”** — Explains that people typically scan, satisfice, and muddle through rather than read interfaces methodically.
- **“Designing for Scanning, Not Reading”** — Argues for clear visual hierarchy, concise copy, and prominent cues that support rapid scanning.
- **“Why Users Like Mindless Choices”** — Shows how familiar conventions reduce decision effort and increase confidence.
- **“Street Signs and Breadcrumbs”** — Treats navigation as orientation: users should understand where they are, where they can go, and how to return.
- **“Usability as Common Courtesy”** — Frames usability as respect for users’ time and attention; the second edition identifies this as one of its three new chapters.[1]
- **“Web Accessibility, CSS, and You”** — Connects usability with accessibility and standards-based implementation; this is another of the second edition’s new chapters.[1]

## Why It Matters to Innovators

- It establishes **cognitive friction** as a product risk: when users must interpret basic interface mechanics, adoption and task completion suffer. This is a practical foundation for [[concepts/Customer Effort]] and [[concepts/Minimum Viable Product|Minimum Viable Product]].
- It shifts usability debates away from design preference and toward observable user behavior and real usability problems.[3] That makes it useful for [[concepts/Jobs-to-be-Done|Jobs-to-be-Done]] and evidence-led product decisions.
- Its emphasis on familiar conventions supports a strong innovation lesson: novelty belongs in the value proposition, not necessarily in basic navigation, labeling, or interaction patterns.
- The book treats usability as an organizational practice rather than a final polish step, helping teams diagnose where internal complexity has leaked into the customer experience.
- Its companion methodology in *Rocket Surgery Made Easy* reinforces the value of lightweight, recurring tests instead of expensive research programs.[10]

## Best Starting Points

- [*Don’t Make Me Think!* — first edition](https://books.google.com/books/about/Don_t_Make_Me_Think.html?id=G_f5dFBGcZMC) — Start here for the original, concise statement of Krug’s usability philosophy.[3]
- [*Don’t Make Me Think, Revisited*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Don_t_Make_Me_Think_Revisited.html?id=qahpAgAAQBAJ) — Use this edition for the expanded treatment of accessibility, organizational pressure, and contemporary web practice.[2]
- [“Usability as common courtesy”] — Begin with this argument when the problem is customer frustration, abandonment, or unnecessary effort.[1]
- [*Rocket Surgery Made Easy*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Rocket_Surgery_Made_Easy.html?id=VzbimAEACAAJ) — Follow with this practical guide when the goal is to establish a lightweight usability-testing habit.[10]

"Don't Make Me Think" by Steve Krug is a foundational book on **UI and UX design** that emphasizes the core principle: user interfaces should be so clear and self-explanatory that users can accomplish their goals *without having to think* about how to use them. [^k4pvfr] [^evb6m5] [^e6jxzr] Krug argues that **usability** is about making digital products intuitive, efficient, and enjoyable by minimizing friction and confusion for users.

Key principles and supporting details:

- **Clarity over cleverness**: Interfaces must be obvious and self-explanatory. Users should never have to puzzle over what to do next; each element’s purpose should be immediately clear. [^k4pvfr] [^evb6m5]
  
  ![Relevant diagram or illustration related to the topic — e.g., a flow illustrating a user’s effortless navigation through a simple web interface, highlighting minimal cognitive load.](https://readingraphics.com/uploads/2020/01/Dont-Make-Me-Think-Revisited_3-Laws-of-Usability.png)

- **[[concepts/Just Good Enough|Satisficing]] behavior**: People look for quick, workable solutions rather than optimal ones. Good design leverages this by guiding users directly to their goals without unnecessary choices or clutter. [^k4pvfr] [^e6jxzr]

- **Navigation must be simple**: Effective navigation and site structures help users quickly find content or complete actions. Krug advocates for straightforward menus, logical paths, and visible cues. [^971hya]
  
  ![Practical example or use case visualization — e.g., a screenshot comparing a confusing menu with an improved, streamlined navigation bar.](https://sekarwrites.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/IMG_0301.jpg)

- **[[Usability Testing]] is essential**: The book stresses the importance of regular, practical usability testing. This can be done easily and inexpensively, allowing teams to iterate and catch issues early. [^k4pvfr] [^evb6m5] Krug’s sequel, "Rocket Surgery Made Easy," expands on testing methods.

- **Mobile and accessibility considerations**: Later editions include guidance for mobile interfaces, reinforcing that all users benefit from ease of use, not just desktop users. [^k4pvfr] [^e6jxzr]

- **Qualities of great UX**: The text outlines essential qualities: learnability, memorability, effectiveness, efficiency, desirability, and delight. [^aqppr5] A usable product should be easy to learn, remember, and use performantly and pleasantly.

  ![Additional supporting visual content — e.g., a checklist or flowchart of core usability questions (Is it learnable? Memorable? Effective? etc.) from the book.](https://i.ytimg.com/vi/gyjV8oY9_xQ/maxresdefault.jpg)

Other notable aspects:

- The book is intentionally concise and approachable, aimed at a wide audience beyond designers, including executives and developers. [^k4pvfr] [^evb6m5]
- Its advice is illustrated with humor, real-world examples (notably Amazon.com), and practical exercises, such as critiquing website layouts. [^aqppr5]
- Krug emphasizes the importance of putting yourself in the user’s shoes and designing from their perspective rather than the developer’s. [^971hya]

By championing simplicity and continuous user testing, "Don't Make Me Think" remains a cornerstone in teaching both the *what* and *why* of good UX and UI design.
