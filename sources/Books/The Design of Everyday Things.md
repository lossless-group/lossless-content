# The Design of Everyday Things

- ![Cover of the revised and expanded edition of *The Design of Everyday Things*](https://m.media-amazon.com/images/S/compressed.photo.goodreads.com/books/1442460745i/840.jpg)
- _A foundational design book that helps innovators see usability problems as design failures—not user failures._
- This source is a **book** by cognitive scientist and designer Donald A. Norman, first published in 1988 and later revised and expanded in 2013.[2][8] Norman is a co-founder of Nielsen Norman Group, a former Apple Fellow, and a longtime researcher and educator in cognitive science and design.[1][3] Consultants return to it because it provides a durable vocabulary for diagnosing why products confuse, frustrate, or mislead people.

## Type and Format

- **Type:** This source is a book.
- **Format details:** The revised and expanded edition was published by MIT Press in 2013 and runs to 347 pages; other editions were published by Basic Books at approximately 368–384 pages.[2][6][8] The revision adds updated material and a new introduction by Norman.[7]
- **Where it lives:** [MIT Press](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262525671/the-design-of-everyday-things/) · [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Design_of_Everyday_Things.html?id=heCtnQEACAAJ)

## The People Behind It

- **Donald A. Norman** is a cognitive scientist, designer, author, and educator who founded the Cognitive Science Department at the University of California, San Diego.[1]
- Norman later joined Apple as an Apple Fellow and helped establish its User Experience Office.[1]
- He co-founded Nielsen Norman Group with Jakob Nielsen in 1998; the organization identifies Norman as the person who coined the term “user experience.”[3]
- Norman also founded UC San Diego’s Design Lab and remains associated with Nielsen Norman Group as a board member.[1][4]
- His broader contribution is connecting cognitive science, human factors, and product design into a practical account of how people interact with systems.[1][3]

## Catalog of Notable Works

- **“The Psychopathology of Everyday Things”** — Introduces the central problem: ordinary objects become difficult when their design fails to communicate how they work.
- **“The Psychology of Everyday Actions”** — Explains the gap between users’ goals, intentions, actions, and interpretations of results.
- **“Knowledge in the Head and in the World”** — Argues that good design distributes necessary knowledge between the user and the environment rather than requiring memorization.
- **“Knowing What to Do”** — Develops the role of discoverability, affordances, signifiers, mappings, and constraints in guiding action.
- **“To Err Is Human”** — Reframes mistakes as predictable consequences of system design and emphasizes designing for recovery.
- **“The Design Challenge”** — Examines the organizational and commercial pressures that make usable design difficult to sustain.
- **“User-Centered Design”** — Makes the case for designing around users’ needs, capabilities, contexts, and mental models.

## Why It Matters to Innovators

- It installs a powerful diagnostic principle: when many users make the same mistake, investigate the design before blaming the users.[2][7] This is useful for [[content-areas/AI-Factories-Datacenters/Concepts/Root-Cause Analysis|Root-Cause Analysis]] and [[concepts/Customer Discovery|Customer Discovery]].
- Norman’s concepts of **affordances, signifiers, mappings, constraints, feedback, and conceptual models** give teams a shared vocabulary for evaluating prototypes and service experiences.
- The book explains why intuitive products are rarely “self-explanatory” by magic; they make intended actions visible and align the system with users’ existing mental models.
- Its treatment of error supports [[concepts/Fault-Tolerant Design]]: products should prevent predictable mistakes, make failures legible, and enable recovery rather than punish users.
- It connects directly to [[concepts/Human-Centered Design]], [[concepts/Usability Testing]], and [[concepts/Minimum Viable Product|Minimum Viable Product]] by showing that early prototypes should test not only desirability, but also whether users can correctly understand and operate the solution.

## Best Starting Points

- [The Design of Everyday Things — Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Design_of_Everyday_Things.html?id=heCtnQEACAAJ) — the canonical book record and the best entry point to the full argument.[2]
- [The Design of Everyday Things — MIT Press](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262525671/the-design-of-everyday-things/) — publisher page for the revised and expanded edition.[8]
- **“The Psychopathology of Everyday Things”** — start here for the book’s core critique of confusing objects.
- **“Knowing What to Do”** — the most useful chapter for translating the theory into interface and product decisions.
- **“User-Centered Design”** — the strongest entry point for teams building products, services, or workflows around real user behavior.
