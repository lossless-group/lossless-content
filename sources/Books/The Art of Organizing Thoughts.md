# The Art of Organizing Thoughts

- _A compact Japanese classic on turning information overload into independent, deliberate thinking._
- This source is a **book** by Japanese critic, essayist, and scholar Shigehiko Toyama (1923–2020), first published in Japanese in 1983 and issued in an English edition by Penguin Books in 2026. [^gkyb2c] [^ncoi9u] A consultant returns to it for its practical distinction between merely accumulating knowledge and cultivating original thought. [^gkyb2c]

## The People Behind It

- **Shigehiko Toyama** was a Japanese critic, essayist, and Doctor of Literature who taught at Tokyo University of Education and Ochanomizu University. [^ncoi9u]
- Toyama lived from 1923 to 2020 and was described by the publisher as a celebrated scholar and teacher. [^ncoi9u]
- **Arthur Reiji Morris** translated the English edition. [^gkyb2c]
- The English publication represents Toyama’s earlier work to contemporary readers, framing its advice as particularly relevant in an age of artificial intelligence. [^gkyb2c]

## Catalog of Notable Works

The available source presents this book through its major arguments rather than a verified chapter list:

- **Organizing knowledge rather than accumulating it** — Knowledge alone does not become wisdom; it must be sorted, reconstructed, and connected to existing understanding. [^gkyb2c] [^lt0n1q]
- **Cultivating the “airplane” mentality** — Schools and workplaces often produce “gliders” who wait for external direction, while innovation requires self-propelled thinkers. [^gkyb2c]
- **Letting ideas ferment** — Problems may benefit from rest, sleep, and time away from deliberate effort rather than constant information intake. [^gkyb2c] [^lt0n1q]
- **Using selective forgetting** — A clear mind depends not only on retaining information but also on discarding what is irrelevant. [^gkyb2c]
- **Protecting morning thinking time** — The book recommends using the morning as a period for thinking with one’s own mind before consuming additional information. [^lt0n1q]
- **Building external thought stores** — Cards, notebooks, and unread books function as reservoirs that allow ideas to be revisited and recombined. [^lt0n1q]

## Why It Matters to Innovators

- It diagnoses a common innovation failure: treating more information as a substitute for better judgment. The book argues that information becomes useful only after it is summarized, paraphrased, reconstructed, and connected to other knowledge. [^gkyb2c] [^lt0n1q]
- It offers a mental model for [[concepts/Independent Thinking]]: the “airplane” thinker generates momentum internally instead of waiting for instructions or market signals. [^gkyb2c]
- Its emphasis on incubation supports [[concepts/Creative Friction]] and [[client-content/AI-Camp/Deliberate Practice]] by treating pauses, sleep, and distance from a problem as part of the thinking process rather than wasted time. [^gkyb2c] [^lt0n1q]
- Its card-and-notebook practices provide a low-tech foundation for [[concepts/Explainers for Tooling/Personal Knowledge Management|Personal Knowledge Management]] and [[concepts/Second Brain]] systems. [^lt0n1q]
- For innovation teams, the book distinguishes **input**, **processing**, and **synthesis**—a useful way to diagnose whether a team is learning, merely collecting research, or generating genuinely new frames. [^gkyb2c] [^lt0n1q]
